On the morning of 1 January 1925, in Corcoran Hall at George Washington University, Henry Norris Russell of Princeton stood up to read a paper that was not his. The American Astronomical Society had been meeting in Washington since 30 December, alongside more than 4,000 other scientists. A few hours earlier, at midnight, the astronomers had given up astronomical reckoning, in which the day began at noon, for civil reckoning, which starts it at midnight like everyone else's.[^pa1925]

The paper was bigger news. Its author, Edwin Hubble of Mount Wilson Observatory, had found Cepheids, stars that brighten and fade on a steady beat, in the Andromeda and Triangulum nebulae, and used them to derive, in the Society's report, "distances approaching one million light years for each".[^pa1925] It went on to share a $1,000 prize for an outstanding paper at the meeting. A million light-years was far too far for anything to belong to the Milky Way. The spiral nebulae were galaxies in their own right. The idea was 175 years old, and for the previous few decades the best-informed astronomers had thought it wrong.

## Clouds that were not comets

Thomas Wright of Durham published *An Original Theory or New Hypothesis of the Universe* in London in 1750. He explained the Milky Way as our view from inside a great layer of stars, and in the ninth of its letters he went further. The faint cloudy spots that telescopes showed, the nebulae (Latin for mists), were in all likelihood other creations beyond our own, too far away for any telescope to split into stars.[^wright1750]

The idea reached Königsberg through a review in a Hamburg journal in 1751, and Immanuel Kant took it up.[^kant1755] In 1755 he pointed to the pale elliptical nebulae described by the French mathematician Pierre-Louis Moreau de Maupertuis. A flat system of stars seen from far away, Kant argued, would look exactly like that: round face-on, oval when tilted, faint because distant. The nebulae were other world-systems, other Milky Ways.[^kant1755] It was a guess. Nobody could measure the distance of even the nearest star until 1838 ([How far are the stars?](#/learn/how-far-are-the-stars)).

::: myth Kant coined the phrase "island universe".
The phrase appears nowhere in his 1755 book. He called the nebulae *Weltordnungen* (world-systems) and *Milchstraßen* (Milky Ways). The only islands in the text are islands on Earth, such as Jamaica, in a passage about sea breezes.[^kant1755]
:::

Charles Messier just wanted the nebulae out of his way. He hunted comets from Paris, and the sky was littered with faint patches that looked like comets but never moved. In 1758, searching in vain for Halley's comet along a wrongly computed path, he picked up a different comet, and while following it he found one of these patches in Taurus. It became the first entry on his list.[^sedsbio][^lhmessier] The list reached 45 objects in 1774 and 103 in 1781.[^sedsmcat] Andromeda went in as number 31 in 1764, eight centuries after the Persian astronomer al-Sufi described it as a little cloud.[^sedsm31] On 13 October 1773, following a comet, Messier found number 51, which he logged as a very faint nebula with no stars in it.[^sedsm51]

In the spring of 1845 William Parsons, third Earl of Rosse, turned a new telescope on M51 at Birr Castle in Ireland. Its metal mirror, six feet (1.8 m) across and cast in his own foundry, made it the largest reflector in the world; the locals called it the Leviathan of Parsonstown.[^lhrosse] In it M51 became a whirlpool, with arms curling out of a bright centre.[^sedsm51] Spirals look like something turning, which suited either side of the argument: a disc of stars, or a cloud of gas collapsing to form a new star and its planets.

::: see-it go:whirlpool
M51, the Whirlpool Galaxy, is about 28 million light-years away.[^mcquinn2016m51] Skyfold draws it as a model scaled to its measured size and tilt: its arms are typical ones, not a map of the two Rosse drew in 1845, and the small companion galaxy at the tip of one of them, which Messier's friend Pierre Méchain spotted in 1781, is not drawn.[^sedsm51]
:::

Then the balance tipped the other way. In 1864 William Huggins, at his private observatory in London, pointed a spectroscope, which spreads light into its colours, at the nebulae. Some showed a few bright lines, the mark of a thin glowing gas ([What stars are made of](#/learn/what-stars-are-made-of) explains why). Andromeda gave a continuous spectrum, which a crowd of stars produces, but so does any dense hot body.[^sedsm31][^lhhuggins] On 20 August 1885 Ernst Hartwig, at Dorpat in what is now Estonia, noticed a new star near the centre of the Andromeda nebula. It reached magnitude 6, the limit of the naked eye; on the magnitude scale, bigger numbers mean fainter.[^sedsm31] If Andromeda were a galaxy as big as ours, one star had briefly outshone a large part of it. That seemed impossible. Stars that destroy themselves that brightly were named supernovae only in 1934, by Walter Baade and Fritz Zwicky.[^lhbaade]

By 1890 the verdict seemed in. In *The System of the Stars*, Agnes Clerke wrote that no competent thinker could now hold "any single nebula to be a star system of co-ordinate rank with the Milky Way".[^clerke1890]

## Speeds nobody expected

The man who reopened the case was not looking for galaxies. In 1909 Percival Lowell set Vesto Slipher, at his observatory in Flagstaff, Arizona, to photographing the spectra of spiral nebulae, which Lowell hoped were young solar systems; by 1912 Slipher was trying to measure the motions of the brightest of them.[^oraif2013][^lhslipher] Light was the problem. Andromeda is easy to see with the naked eye, Slipher explained, yet spread into a spectrum its light is extremely feeble. He fitted one very dense prism and a short camera to the observatory's 24-inch (61 cm) lens telescope. His first plate, on 17 September 1912, took 6 hours and 50 minutes.[^slipher1913]

Andromeda's spectral lines, dark lines at fixed colours that mark particular atoms, had shifted towards the blue. That is the Doppler effect: light from a source moving towards you arrives squeezed to shorter wavelengths, and light from one moving away is stretched to longer ones.

$$v = c\,\frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda}$$

In words: the speed of a source towards or away from you equals the speed of light times the fraction by which its spectral lines have moved. Blue means approaching, red means receding. This simple form holds when the speed is small compared with light's; [What you would see near the speed of light](#/learn/seeing-near-light-speed) deals with the fast cases.

Slipher's four plates, taken by the end of December, gave 284, 296, 308 and 301 km/s, all towards us, and he rounded the mean to 300 km/s.[^slipher1913] Divide by the speed of light, 299,792 km/s, and the fractional shift is 0.0010. A hydrogen line at 434 nm moves by 0.43 nm, one part in a thousand, measured on a faint smudge of spectrum. It was, Slipher noted, "the greatest hitherto observed". He could find no other cause for the shift, and floated the idea that the speeding nebula had struck a dark star and set off the flare of 1885.[^slipher1913]

Andromeda turned out to be the odd one. By 1914 Slipher had 15 spirals, 12 of them moving away; by 1917 he had 25, with 21 receding, some at 1,100 km/s.[^lhslipher] Stars near the Sun move at tens of kilometres per second. Things moving at a thousand were hard to keep inside the Milky Way. Why almost all of them were moving away is a bigger story, told in [The expanding universe](#/learn/the-expanding-universe).

## A clock that tells brightness

In 1903 Henrietta Swan Leavitt went back to work at Harvard College Observatory, at 30 cents an hour. She was losing her hearing. Her job was to find variable stars on glass photographic plates, and she was given the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, photographed from Harvard's southern station in Peru; from Massachusetts the Clouds never rise.[^lhleavitt] In 1908 she published a catalogue of 1,777 variable stars in the two Clouds.[^leavitt1912]

For a few of them she had measured periods, the time for one cycle of brightening and fading, and the brighter stars took longer, though she felt there were too few to be sure. By 1912 she had periods for 25 stars in the Small Cloud, from 1.25 days to 127, published in Harvard Circular 173 under the name of the director, Edward Pickering, whose first sentence credits the work to her.[^leavitt1912] Her argument took two lines. The stars in the Cloud are all at nearly the same distance from us, so if one looks brighter than another, it really is brighter. Therefore "their periods are apparently associated with their actual emission of light".[^leavitt1912]

She plotted brightness against the logarithm of the period, and the points fell on a straight line. A one-paragraph guide to logarithms: the logarithm of a number answers the question *ten to what power gives this?* So $\log_{10} 10 = 1$ and $\log_{10} 100 = 2$, and numbers in between have logarithms in between: $\log_{10} 30 = 1.48$, because $10^{1.48} = 30$. Multiplying a number by ten adds exactly one to its logarithm, which turns huge ranges into small, even steps.

$$M = a\,\log_{10} P + b$$

In words: a Cepheid's absolute magnitude $M$, which measures how bright it really is, lies on a straight line when plotted against the logarithm of its period $P$ in days. The slope $a$ says how much brighter each tenfold increase in period makes a star, and the zero point $b$ ties the line to real brightness. Smaller magnitudes mean brighter, so $a$ is negative.

Leavitt found that the logarithm of the period rose by about 0.48 for each magnitude of brightness.[^leavitt1912] That is $1 \div 0.48 = 2.1$ magnitudes per step of the logarithm, so $a \approx -2.1$. A Cepheid pulsing every 30 days has a logarithm one higher than one pulsing every 3, so it is 2.1 magnitudes brighter. Five magnitudes is a factor of 100, so 2.1 magnitudes is a factor of $10^{2.1/2.5} = 6.9$. Time a Cepheid's pulse and you know its true power; measure how faint it looks and the inverse-square law (twice as far, four times fainter) gives its distance.

The slope came free. The zero point did not, because nobody knew the distance of the Small Cloud. In 1913 the Danish astronomer Ejnar Hertzsprung calibrated the law from the drift across the sky of 13 Cepheids near the Sun and put the Small Cloud 33,000 light-years away, then by far the largest distance measured for any single object.[^baade1956] The modern figure is 204,000 light-years, six times further.[^graczyk2020] In 1918 Harlow Shapley stretched the law to the short-period variables of globular clusters, dense round swarms of stars, joining them to Leavitt's stars through the few long-period Cepheids found in clusters such as Omega Centauri. As Baade later put it, the step looked perfectly straightforward then, and nobody protested.[^baade1956]

Cepheids are named after Delta Cephei, which John Goodricke, a young English astronomer deaf from early childhood, found to vary in 1784; he died at 21.[^openstax] What drives the pulse is a layer of helium inside the star. When that helium is doubly ionised, stripped of both its electrons, it is opaque, so it traps heat and its pressure pushes the layers above it outwards. The star swells and cools, the helium recaptures electrons and turns transparent, the heat escapes, and the star falls back: a valve that keeps opening and shutting.[^fm2010] Bigger, brighter Cepheids are less dense, and a less dense star rings more slowly, so period and brightness rise together.[^fm2010]

## Two talks in Washington

The Great Debate nearly did not happen. In January 1920 Charles Abbot, organising an evening lecture for the National Academy of Sciences, told George Ellery Hale he feared people cared so little about island universes that the subject would fall flat. He was even less keen on Hale's other idea, relativity, which he doubted half a dozen members could follow.[^hoskin1976]

On 26 April 1920, at 8:15 in the evening, Harlow Shapley of Mount Wilson and Heber Curtis of Lick Observatory each spoke for 40 minutes on "The Scale of the Universe". Shapley, angling for a senior post at Harvard and anxious to avoid a public brawl, gave an elementary talk that took more than six pages of typescript to reach the definition of a light-year. Curtis brought a technical case typed out on slides.[^hoskin1976]

Shapley's argument rested on the size of the Milky Way. From the variables in globular clusters he had measured a galaxy 300,000 light-years across, ten times the older figure Curtis defended.[^shapleycurtis1921] Spirals comparable to that would have to be absurdly far away. Adriaan van Maanen at Mount Wilson had measured M101, M81 and M33 turning on photographs taken years apart, which at any great distance would mean impossible speeds; in print Shapley called the rotations fatal to the idea of other galaxies.[^shapleycurtis1921] There were also the novae seen in spirals (a nova is a star that suddenly flares up, then fades), which Shapley thought would have to be far too bright if the spirals were separate galaxies, and photographs of M51 that looked too blue to be made of stars.[^trimble1995] "I prefer to believe that they are not composed of stars at all, but are truly nebulous objects," Shapley said.[^hoskin1976]

Curtis answered point by point. Spirals have spectra like the combined light of a star cluster. They move at 1,200 km/s on average, five times faster than the star clusters. They avoid the band of the Milky Way, and edge-on spirals show dark lanes of dust at their rims; a lane like that in our own galaxy would hide any spirals behind it. Most telling were the novae: sixteen in Andromeda, peaking at about 17th magnitude, where our galaxy's novae peak around 5th. If they were the same kind of star, Andromeda was about 500,000 light-years away, and the 1885 star belonged to a different, far brighter class.[^hoskin1976][^shapleycurtis1921]

Each man was right about something. Shapley's galaxy was too big and Curtis's too small; the Milky Way's disc is about 100,000 light-years across.[^lhcurtis] Shapley was right that the Sun lies far from its centre ([Our galaxy](#/learn/our-galaxy) tells that part), and Curtis was right about the spirals. The historian Michael Hoskin, working from their letters, judged that there was hardly a contest, but that Curtis came out ahead, as Curtis told his family at the time.[^hoskin1976] In 1922 the Estonian astronomer Ernst Öpik did better than either. From the speed at which Andromeda's centre rotates, and assuming its stars shine like ours for their mass, he found a distance of 450,000 parsecs, 1.5 million light-years: a stellar universe comparable with our own.[^opik1922]

::: myth The Great Debate proved that other galaxies exist.
It proved nothing and was hardly a debate: two 40-minute talks at different levels, to an audience of mostly non-astronomers, with no verdict. The long papers usually quoted were written afterwards and published in May 1921. The question stayed open for more than four years.[^hoskin1976]
:::

Van Maanen's rotations did not survive. In 1935, after Hubble, Baade and Seth Nicholson published measurements of their own, van Maanen agreed that his motions should be viewed with reserve.[^vanmaanen1935]

## VAR!

On the night of 5 October 1923 Hubble began an observing run on the 100-inch Hooker telescope at Mount Wilson that lasted into the early hours of the 6th. The seeing, astronomers' word for the steadiness of the air, was poor. On a 45-minute exposure of the Andromeda nebula he found three spots he took for novae and marked each with an N. Comparing earlier plates of the same field, he saw that one brightened and faded far too quickly for a nova. He crossed out its N and wrote "VAR!". The star, now called V1, varies every 31.4 days. It was a Cepheid.[^nasa2011] When Hubble sent Shapley the news with a graph of the star's brightness, Shapley is reported to have told a colleague, "Here is the letter that destroyed my universe."[^nasa2011]

By the end of 1924 Hubble had periods for 12 Cepheids in Andromeda and 22 in Triangulum.[^hubble1925] Put a Cepheid's period into Leavitt's law, calibrated as Shapley had calibrated it, and you have its absolute magnitude $M$. Measure how bright it looks, its apparent magnitude $m$, and the gap between the two gives the distance.

$$d = 10\ \text{pc} \times 10^{(m - M)/5}$$

In words: a star's distance is 10 parsecs times ten raised to one-fifth of the difference between how bright it looks and how bright it really is. Every 5 magnitudes of difference puts the star ten times further away, because it is then 100 times fainter and light fades with the square of distance. A parsec is 3.26 light-years; this is the distance modulus of [How far are the stars?](#/learn/how-far-are-the-stars), turned round.

Hubble found $m - M$ of about 22.3 for both nebulae.[^hubble1925] Then $d = 10 \times 10^{22.3/5} = 10 \times 10^{4.46} = 288{,}000$ pc. Hubble gave about 285,000 parsecs, which the reprint in *The Observatory* converted to 930,000 light-years.[^hubble1925] Even Shapley's Milky Way was only 300,000 light-years across.

Hubble listed his assumptions: that the variables belong to the nebulae, that dust does not dim them seriously, and that Cepheids behave the same everywhere.[^hubble1925] His full paper on Andromeda in 1929 drew on about 350 plates spread over 18 years and 40 Cepheids, and gave 275,000 parsecs, or 900,000 light-years.[^hubble1929]

## The universe doubles

The distance was wrong, and Hubble had half-said so. "The greatest uncertainty is probably in the zero point of Shapley's curve," he wrote in 1925.[^hubble1925]

The clue came from Andromeda's globular clusters. In 1931 Hubble found the brightest of them 1.5 magnitudes fainter than the brightest in our galaxy. Baade, a German astronomer at Mount Wilson, doubted that two galaxies with similar numbers of clusters could differ so much and suspected one of the distances; the two men argued about it on cloudy winter nights on the mountain.[^baade1956] In 1944 Baade, using red-sensitive plates on the 100-inch, resolved Andromeda's two companions and its centre into stars for the first time. Their brightest stars were red, like those of globular clusters, not blue like the brightest stars near the Sun. Galaxies, he concluded, hold two stellar populations: Population I, like the stars around the Sun, and Population II, like those of globular clusters, now known to be old and poor in heavy elements.[^baade1944][^lhbaade]

Leavitt's Cepheids belong to Population I. The cluster variables Shapley had joined to them belong to Population II, and there was no reason for the two kinds to obey the same law.[^baade1956] The 200-inch telescope at Palomar could test it. If Hubble's distance was right, the RR Lyrae stars of Andromeda, the short-period variables of Population II, should appear at photographic magnitude 22.4, just within reach. Observing from the autumn of 1950, Baade found instead that the brightest Population II stars, which outshine RR Lyrae stars by 1.5 magnitudes, were only just reaching 22.4. The RR Lyrae stars had to be down at 23.9.[^baade1956]

So Leavitt's kind of Cepheid was 1.5 magnitudes brighter than anyone had thought, and since $10^{1.5/5} = 2.0$, every distance measured with them had to be doubled. Baade announced it to the International Astronomical Union in Rome in September 1952. As soon as he finished, Andrew Thackeray of the Radcliffe Observatory rose to say that he and Adriaan Wesselink had just found RR Lyrae stars in a globular cluster of the Small Magellanic Cloud, at magnitude 19 instead of the predicted 17.3.[^baade1956] New electronic measurements of brightness also showed that Hubble's faint magnitudes had been too bright, by another 0.45 magnitudes, a further factor of 1.23.[^baade1956]

The modern figure comes from 55 Cepheids in Andromeda measured with the Hubble Space Telescope and calibrated against Cepheids in the Large Magellanic Cloud: a distance modulus of 24.407, or 761,000 parsecs, give or take 1.5%.[^li2021] That is 2.67 times Hubble's 1925 number. Hubble's picture of the universe survived intact; every galaxy just moved further away and grew.

## Sorting the islands

Once galaxies were galaxies, Hubble sorted them. His 1926 paper "Extra-galactic nebulae" arranged the regular ones in a sequence: smooth ellipticals, from round to flattened; spirals, from those with big bright centres and tight arms to those with small centres and loose, knotty arms; and a parallel sequence of spirals with a bar of stars across the middle. Those that fitted nowhere were irregular. The Swedish astronomer Knut Lundmark published a similar scheme at almost the same time, and Hubble defended his claim to have got there first in a pointed footnote.[^hubble1926][^sandage1989]

In his 1936 book Hubble drew the sequence as a letter Y or, since the two spiral branches run side by side, a tuning fork. He also noted that it closely resembled the order in which the physicist James Jeans had suggested nebulae might evolve.[^hubble1936][^sandage1989] Jeans had already drawn a Y-shaped diagram of Hubble's classes in the late 1920s, in his book *Astronomy and Cosmogony*, and Hubble did not credit it.[^blockfreeman2015]

::: myth Hubble's tuning fork shows how galaxies evolve, from ellipticals into spirals.
Hubble called ellipticals "early" and spirals "late", but warned in 1926 that he used the words "in spite of their temporal connotations", as convenient labels for positions in the sequence.[^hubble1926] If anything, the modern view runs the other way: when big spirals merge, the remnant is expected to look like an elliptical, and that is the predicted fate of the Milky Way and Andromeda if they collide.[^vdm2012c][^nasa2012]
:::

## Too much gravity

Galaxies weigh more than they look. Knut Lundmark had found hints of this in 1930, from the spectra of five galaxies, but the claim that stuck came from Fritz Zwicky, a Swiss astronomer at Caltech, in 1933.[^bertone2018] The Coma Cluster holds about 800 galaxies whose speeds along our line of sight differ by at least 1,500 km/s. A cluster stays together only if its gravity can hold galaxies moving that fast, and Zwicky found it would need a density at least 400 times higher than its starlight implied. If that were confirmed, he wrote, "dark matter exists in much greater density than luminous matter".[^zwicky1933]

Zwicky used a value of the Hubble constant, the expansion rate of the universe ([The expanding universe](#/learn/the-expanding-universe)), about 8 times today's, which inflated his ratio of mass to light (Suns' worth of mass for each Sun's worth of light) by the same factor.[^bertone2018] Divide 400 by 8.3 and you still get about 50. In 1937 he put the average Coma galaxy at no less than 45 billion solar masses, with a ratio of mass to light of about 500, against about 3 for the stars around the Sun. In the same paper he suggested that galaxies might bend the light of more distant objects, acting as gravitational lenses.[^zwicky1937]

In 1959 Franz Kahn and Lodewijk Woltjer, at Princeton, tried the argument on our own neighbourhood. Andromeda and the Milky Way are approaching each other, at 125 km/s by their reckoning. If the two formed close together, then moved apart and started falling back within the age of the universe, the pair must weigh at least 1.8 trillion Suns. They could credit the galaxies and their companions with only 500 billion, and guessed that the rest was hot, thin gas.[^kahnwoltjer1959]

## Galaxies that do not slow down

The planets show what gravity does when nearly all the mass sits in the middle: the further out an orbit, the slower it goes.

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{r}}$$

In words: the speed $v$ of something on a circular orbit is the square root of the gravitational constant $G$ times the mass $M$ inside the orbit, divided by the orbit's radius $r$. With the same mass inside, going four times further out halves the speed. Turned round, $M = v^2 r / G$: measure how fast something orbits and how far out it is, and you have weighed everything inside its orbit.

The Earth orbits the Sun at 29.8 km/s. Neptune is 30 times further out, so it should be slower by a factor of $\sqrt{30} = 5.5$, giving 5.4 km/s, exactly what it does.[^nssdc] A galaxy's stars are spread out, so its rotation speed rises at first, but once you pass most of the starlight it should fall off like the planets. Andromeda's does not. In 1970 Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured 67 glowing clouds of hydrogen out to 24 kpc (kiloparsecs), about 78,000 light-years, from its centre, with an image-tube spectrograph, whose electronic intensifier cut exposure times tenfold.[^rubinford1970] Beyond about 5 kpc the speed fell only slowly, mostly staying above 200 km/s, and the outermost points, near 22 kpc, were not falling at all.[^rubinford1970]

Take 210 km/s ($2.1 \times 10^5$ m/s) at 24 kpc ($7.4 \times 10^{20}$ m). Then $M = (2.1 \times 10^5)^2 \times 7.4 \times 10^{20} \div 6.67 \times 10^{-11} = 4.9 \times 10^{41}$ kg. At $2 \times 10^{30}$ kg per Sun, that is about 250 billion Suns. Rubin and Ford, modelling a flat disc, found 185 billion.[^rubinford1970] The point is the flatness. If $v$ stays the same while $r$ doubles, the mass inside must double too, even where there is almost no light.

::: myth Vera Rubin discovered dark matter in 1970.
The 1970 paper made no such claim. Rubin and Ford reported the mass inside 24 kpc and called any extrapolation beyond it "clearly a matter of taste".[^rubinford1970] Zwicky, Kahn and Woltjer and others had argued for missing mass long before, and radio astronomers were finding flat rotation with the 21 cm line, radio waves given off by cold hydrogen gas.[^bertone2018] In 1975 Morton Roberts and Robert Whitehurst traced Andromeda's rotation at constant speed from 20 to 30 kpc and suggested the extra mass might be faint red dwarf stars.[^roberts1975] What Rubin's group did was make the problem unavoidable: in 1980 they published rotation curves of 21 spiral galaxies, none of them falling, and concluded that all must have significant mass beyond their visible edges.[^rubin1980]
:::

### A collision that pulled them apart

The most direct evidence came in 2006 from two galaxy clusters that passed through each other about 4 billion light-years away, known as 1E 0657-558 or the Bullet Cluster.[^chandra2006] Most of the ordinary matter in a cluster is not in its galaxies but in hundred-million-degree gas between them, which glows in X-rays. In the collision the two gas clouds hit and were slowed, as air slows a thrown ball, while the widely spaced galaxies sailed on. The Chandra X-ray telescope mapped the gas. Douglas Clowe and his colleagues mapped the mass from the way it bends the light of galaxies behind it, the lensing Zwicky had proposed in 1937.[^clowe2006][^chandra2006] The mass sits with the galaxies, not the gas, though the gas outweighs the galaxies. The offset is significant at 8 sigma, a measure of how unlikely it is to be a fluke, far past the 5 sigma physicists usually require, and, Clowe's team argued, no change to the law of gravity alone could explain it.[^clowe2006]

## The neighbourhood

Hubble named our corner of space in 1936. The galactic system, he wrote, belongs to a small group isolated in the general field: the Milky Way and the two Magellanic Clouds, Andromeda with M32 and NGC 205, M33, NGC 6822 and IC 1613, with perhaps a few more hidden by dust. He called it the local group.[^hubble1936] A 2012 census listed over a hundred galaxies within 3 Mpc (megaparsecs, about 10 million light-years), most of them faint dwarfs.[^mcconnachie2012] The Milky Way and Andromeda together weigh about 3 trillion Suns.[^vdm2012b]

::: see-it local-group
Two big spirals dominate the Local Group: the Milky Way and Andromeda, about 2.5 million light-years apart, each with its swarm of satellites. Triangulum sits off to one side. From this far they are faint smudges and most of the satellites are too faint to see; the Bodies list names every one.
:::

The Large Magellanic Cloud is 49.6 kpc (161,700 light-years) away, a distance measured to 1% in 2019 from 20 pairs of stars that eclipse each other.[^pietrzynski2019] In 2006 Nitya Kallivayalil and colleagues used the Hubble Space Telescope to track the Clouds across the sky and found them moving about 100 km/s faster than expected, close to the Milky Way's escape speed, the speed needed to break free of its gravity. Gurtina Besla and colleagues then argued that, for a Milky Way of the mass that standard cosmological models suggested, the Clouds must be on their first pass, not old satellites.[^kallivayalil2006][^besla2007] The Large Cloud weighs about 140 billion Suns, enough to bend streams of stars in our galaxy's outskirts.[^erkal2019]

::: see-it go:lmc
The Large Magellanic Cloud, 161,700 light-years away and probably on its first pass by the Milky Way. The gas that it and the Small Cloud have lost trails behind them as the Magellanic Stream.
:::

In 1974 Don Mathewson and two colleagues in Australia, surveying the southern sky for hydrogen, traced a filament of gas from the Clouds down to the south pole of the Milky Way. They joined it to a filament other radio astronomers had found shortly before and named the whole 180-degree arc the Magellanic Stream.[^mathewson1974] By 2010 the Stream and the Leading Arm, which runs ahead of the Clouds, had been traced over at least 200 degrees.[^nidever2010] Measured against the light of distant quasars, the brilliant cores of far-off galaxies, the whole system holds about 2 billion solar masses of gas, more than the neutral hydrogen left in both Clouds combined. Most of it is ionised, its atoms stripped of electrons, and so invisible to the radio surveys that found the Stream. It covers about a quarter of the sky.[^fox2014]

::: myth Andromeda is the most distant thing you can see without a telescope.
The Triangulum Galaxy, M33, is about 3 million light-years away and shines at magnitude 5.7; keen-eyed observers can see it under exceptionally dark skies.[^nasam33] Hubble's 1925 paper opens by calling M31 and M33 "the only spirals that can be seen with the naked eye".[^hubble1925]
:::

## Andromeda is coming

Much of Slipher's 300 km/s was our own doing. The Sun is carried round the Milky Way at more than 200 km/s, partly towards Andromeda.[^kahnwoltjer1959] Take that away and Andromeda approaches the centre of the Milky Way at 109 km/s.[^vdm2012b] At that speed the 761 kpc gap would close in 6.8 billion years; gravity speeds the fall, so the real answer is sooner.

Whether they actually hit depends on Andromeda's sideways motion, which is far harder to measure. By the rule in [How far are the stars?](#/learn/how-far-are-the-stars), something $d$ parsecs away moving sideways at $v$ km/s drifts $v \div (4.74\,d)$ arcseconds a year, where an arcsecond is 1/3,600 of a degree. A sideways speed of 100 km/s at 761,000 parsecs gives $100 \div (4.74 \times 761{,}000) = 0.000028$ arcseconds a year. In 2012 Roeland van der Marel and colleagues used Hubble Space Telescope measurements of that drift and found the sideways speed small, 17 km/s.[^vdm2012b] Their simulations put the first close pass 3.9 billion years away and a merger at 5.9 billion, with a 41% chance of a direct hit.[^vdm2012c] NASA announced a head-on collision.[^nasa2012]

ESA's star-mapping satellite Gaia later measured the drift from Andromeda's own stars and got a larger sideways speed, 82 km/s, give or take 31.[^salomon2021] In 2025 Till Sawala and colleagues ran 100,000 simulations, varying 22 quantities within their uncertainties and adding the next two biggest members of the Local Group. Triangulum pulls the pair towards a merger; the Large Magellanic Cloud, whose orbit runs at right angles to theirs, pulls them away from one. The result: close to a 50% chance that the Milky Way and Andromeda do not merge in the next 10 billion years, and about a 2% chance of a head-on collision in 4 to 5 billion. When they do merge, it typically takes about 8 billion years. The Large Magellanic Cloud, by contrast, typically falls into the Milky Way in about 1.3 billion years.[^sawala2025][^esa2025]

::: myth When the Milky Way and Andromeda collide, their stars will smash into each other.
Stars are so far apart compared with their size that almost none will touch; NASA's summary of the 2012 study says the stars will not collide, only be thrown into new orbits.[^nasa2012] Shrink the Sun to a grain of sand half a millimetre across and the nearest star, Proxima Centauri, is another grain 14 km away.
:::

## What comes next

Status as of late September 2026.

### Gaia Data Release 4

**Status:** scheduled for 2 December 2026, drawing on 66 months of observations.[^gaiarel] A longer run pins down motions better, and Sawala's team named the uncertain motions and masses of the Local Group's galaxies as the limit on the collision forecast.[^sawala2025]

### Euclid

**Status:** operating. ESA's Euclid launched on 1 July 2023 and began routine observations on 14 February 2024. It is mapping billions of galaxies out to 10 billion light-years over more than a third of the sky, using lensing to map dark matter.[^euclidov] Its first large release, covering about 1,900 square degrees, is due in November 2026, and the complete first release, with the lensing results, in mid-2027; ESA has not yet given exact dates.[^eucliddr1]

### The Rubin Observatory's survey

**Status:** running. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile began its ten-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time at the end of June 2026.[^rubin2026] Among its dark-matter tests, its designers listed counting the faint dwarf galaxies around the Milky Way and looking for kinks in streams of stars disturbed by passing clumps of dark matter.[^ivezic2019]

### The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope

**Status:** commissioning. Roman launched on 30 August 2026 and switched on its 300-megapixel camera in September; science operations are due by early 2027.[^romanblog] Its field of view is at least 100 times Hubble's.[^romanov]

### The Extremely Large Telescope

**Status:** under construction in Chile. ESO plans first light in 2029 and the first science observations in December 2030; dates that far out can slip.[^elt]

### Open questions

Nobody knows what dark matter is; as of September 2026 no experiment has confirmed a dark-matter particle. Andromeda's sideways motion is still uncertain enough to leave the collision close to a coin toss. And the census of the Local Group is unfinished; Rubin's survey was designed in part to find more of its faint dwarfs.[^mcconnachie2012][^ivezic2019]
