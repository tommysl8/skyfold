On Friday 11 January 1935, at a meeting of the Royal Astronomical Society in London, Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar explained why a dead star above a certain mass has nothing left to hold it up. He was 24, a Fellow of Trinity College, Cambridge, and a friend of Arthur Eddington.[^chandrabio] After a comment from Edward Milne, Eddington followed the star all the way down. It would go on radiating and shrinking, he said, until its radius was a few kilometres and its gravity held in its own light, "and the star can at last find peace".[^obs1935]

It was a fair description of a black hole, offered to throw it out: there should be a law of Nature, Eddington told the meeting, to stop a star behaving in so absurd a way.[^obs1935] There is no such law. Chandrasekhar shared the 1983 Nobel Prize in Physics,[^nobel1983] and black holes have since been weighed, pictured and heard colliding.

The shape of space and time around a black hole that does not spin has been known exactly since 1916. It tells you what one looks like from close by, how slowly your clock runs there and what you would see as you fell in. Skyfold draws all of it, for Sagittarius A\* (Sgr A\*) at the centre of our galaxy and for thirty-nine other black holes.

## Dark stars

In May 1783 John Michell, a clergyman at Thornhill in Yorkshire, sent a long paper to his friend Henry Cavendish, who laid it before the Royal Society; it was printed in 1784.[^michell1784][^montgomery2009] Like Newton, Michell took light to be a stream of particles, slowed by gravity as they climb away from a star. A star as dense as the Sun but with 500 times its radius, he worked out, would turn its own light back and could never be seen. It need not stay hidden: if a visible star orbited one, astronomers "might still perhaps from the motions of these revolving bodies infer the existence of the central ones".[^michell1784] That is how most of the black holes below were found.

Pierre-Simon Laplace made the same suggestion in 1796: a star as dense as the Earth, with 250 times the Sun's diameter, would let none of its light reach us, so the largest bodies in the universe might be invisible.[^laplace1796] He published a proof in 1799 at the request of the astronomer Franz Xaver von Zach, but the passage was gone from his book by its 1808 edition.[^montgomery2009]

Both arguments rest on escape speed, the slowest throw that never falls back: $\sqrt{2GM/R}$ from a body of mass $M$ and radius $R$, where $G$ is the gravitational constant. From the Sun's surface it is 618 km/s, and light is 485 times faster. At a fixed density escape speed grows in step with the radius, so Michell's 500 was close. Setting it equal to $c$, the speed of light, gives a radius of $2GM/c^2$, which by a coincidence of factors is exactly the radius general relativity gives.

A dark star is still not a black hole. Light leaving it would rise, stop and fall back like a ball, visible to anyone close by, and a rocket could climb away slowly without ever reaching escape speed. Michell's and Laplace's stars were also huge and no denser than the Sun or the Earth, while the black holes found in pairs of stars are tiny and extremely dense.[^montgomery2009] The giants at the centres of galaxies are another matter. Michell's star would have 125 million times the Sun's mass, in the range of the supermassive black holes found two centuries later, and the mass of M87\*, spread through a ball the size of its horizon, would be thinner than air.

## A solution from the front

Einstein presented the finished theory of general relativity in November 1915: mass and energy curve space and time, and falling bodies follow the straightest paths they can through it.[^nobelpop2020] Karl Schwarzschild, director of the Astrophysical Observatory at Potsdam, had volunteered for the army in August 1914 and served in Belgium and France before being sent to Russia. There, within weeks of Einstein's paper, he found the first exact solution of its equations: the curved spacetime around a single round mass.[^mactutor][^nobelpop2020] He sent it to Einstein, who replied: "I had not expected that one could formulate the exact solution of the problem in such a simple way."[^mactutor] The paper was presented to the Prussian Academy of Sciences on 13 January 1916.[^schwarzschild1916] Schwarzschild had caught pemphigus, a rare skin disease, in Russia. He was sent home in March and died on 11 May 1916, aged 42.[^mactutor]

The solution holds outside any round body that does not spin, and very nearly outside the Sun. It has a special radius, now named after him:

$$r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2}$$

In words: the Schwarzschild radius is twice the gravitational constant times the mass, divided by the square of the speed of light. Inside it lies the black hole.

For the Sun, $GM = 1.327 \times 10^{20}$ m³/s² and $c^2 = 8.988 \times 10^{16}$ m²/s², so $r_s = 2 \times 1.327 \times 10^{20} / (8.988 \times 10^{16}) = 2{,}953$ m. Squeeze the Sun into a ball 5.9 km across and it becomes a black hole; for the Earth the radius is 8.9 mm. The radius grows in step with the mass, so Sgr A\*, with 4.297 million times the Sun's mass, has $r_s = 12.7$ million km, 18 times the Sun's radius, and M87\*, with 6.5 billion, has 128 au, more than three times Pluto's average distance from the Sun.[^gravity2022][^eht2019vi]

At that radius Schwarzschild's formulas gave a clock rate of zero and terms that grew without limit, and for decades most physicists, Schwarzschild among them, took it that nothing real could be squeezed so small.[^mactutor][^nobelpop2020]

## The star that cannot find peace

A white dwarf, the ember a star like the Sun leaves behind, is held up by its electrons. Quantum physics forbids two electrons to share a state, so crushed together they must move faster, and their motion pushes back. In a heavy enough white dwarf they come close to the speed of light and can push no harder. In a note dated 12 November 1930 Chandrasekhar put the largest possible mass at 0.91 times the Sun's for the make-up he assumed; the modern value is about 1.4.[^chandra1931] Above it, the electrons cannot hold the star up.

In February 1939 Robert Oppenheimer and George Volkoff found that in their simple model a core of neutrons, packed tighter still, also had a limit, about three-quarters of the Sun's mass, above which it would contract without end.[^ov1939] That September Oppenheimer and Hartland Snyder followed such a collapse. To someone riding on the star it is over in about a day. From far away the star shrinks towards its Schwarzschild radius without ever reaching it, its light reddening, and that light can escape only "over a progressively narrower range of angles".[^os1939] Where that range ends, a black hole's shadow begins. The paper was published on 1 September 1939, the day Germany invaded Poland.

Einstein argued the same autumn that no real body could be squeezed inside the Schwarzschild radius.[^norton2024] As the announcement of the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics put it, he did not believe that black holes exist.[^nobel2020]

::: myth Nothing ever falls into a black hole, because time stops at the horizon.
Seen from far away, a falling star or spaceship does slow as it nears the horizon, reddening and fading and never quite arriving, which is why black holes were once called frozen stars.[^nasa2024] That is the time of distant clocks, which Einstein himself distinguished from the time on a falling clock.[^norton2024] A clock riding along ticks normally and crosses the horizon in a finite time: about a day for Oppenheimer and Snyder's star,[^os1939] and 864 s from ten horizon radii of Sgr A\* for someone dropped from rest far away.
:::

## A one-way surface

In 1958 David Finkelstein rewrote Schwarzschild's solution in new coordinates and showed that nothing special happens to anything crossing the radius: it is a one-way membrane that influences can cross inwards but never outwards.[^finkelstein1958] In 1960 Martin Kruskal drew the whole of that spacetime, inside and out, as one map.[^kruskal1960] The surface is now called the event horizon.

These solutions were perfectly round and smooth, and real stars are not; perhaps an untidy collapse would miss the centre and bounce. In January 1965 Roger Penrose proved that once a collapse has gone far enough, a singularity, where general relativity itself breaks down, must form whatever the shape. Half the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics went to him, for showing that the formation of black holes is a robust prediction of general relativity.[^penrose1965][^nobel2020][^nobelpop2020]

The geometry round a spinning black hole had already been found. In 1963 Roy Kerr, a New Zealander at the University of Texas, was working out the angular momentum of a new solution, with Alfred Schild puffing at a pipe in the room, and when the answer came out Kerr told him: "Its rotating!" (his spelling).[^kerr2007][^kerr1963] That December a symposium in Dallas met to puzzle over quasars such as 3C 273, which Maarten Schmidt had shown to be far away and so enormously bright;[^kerr2007][^schmidt1963] their power, it turned out, comes from matter falling into giant black holes.[^nobelpop2020] Once a black hole has settled down, its outside is fixed by three numbers: its mass, its spin and its electric charge.[^hamilton]

The words "black holes" first appeared in print in *Science News-Letter* on 18 January 1964, in Ann Ewing's report of a meeting in Cleveland, and nobody knows who said them first.[^siegfried2013] John Wheeler used the name in a lecture in New York on 29 December 1967, and after that everyone did.[^herdeiro2018]

::: myth Black holes suck in everything around them.
From far enough away a black hole pulls like anything else of the same mass. Replace the Sun with a black hole of the same mass and the planets would keep their orbits, in the cold.[^nasabh] Sgr A\*'s pull matters only within a few parsecs of the centre of the Galaxy; [Our galaxy](#/learn/our-galaxy) has the numbers.
:::

## Finding one

Gas spiralling into a black hole grows hot enough to shine in X-rays, which the atmosphere blocks.[^boltonobit] In 1964 Geiger counters carried above the air by a rocket from New Mexico found an X-ray source in Cygnus, Cygnus X-1.[^icrar2021] After Uhuru, the first X-ray astronomy satellite, went up from Kenya in December 1970,[^uhuru] Cygnus X-1 was pinned to a ninth-magnitude blue star, HDE 226868.[^boltonobit][^murdin2024]

In 1971 Louise Webster and Paul Murdin, at the Isaac Newton Telescope in Sussex, and Tom Bolton, at the David Dunlap Observatory near Toronto, tracked that star's speed. It swung to and fro every 5.6 days around a heavy companion that gave off no light, most likely a black hole, as their papers in *Nature* reported early in 1972.[^webster1972][^bolton1972][^boltonobit] Bolton's number plate read CYG X-1.[^boltonobit] In 1974 Stephen Hawking bet Kip Thorne that it was not a black hole, and in 1990 he conceded.[^icrar2021] In 2021 a radio parallax put it 7,200 light-years away, which made the black hole 21 solar masses and its blue supergiant 41, a quarter of an au apart; its spin is claimed at more than 99.8% of the maximum.[^millerjones2021][^icrar2021] A 2025 study of the supergiant's light gives a lighter black hole, 13 to 18 solar masses depending on how the orbit is tilted.[^ramachandran2025]

Most black holes swallow nothing and give off nothing, but Michell's method finds them anyway. In 2022 Kareem El-Badry and colleagues found a Sun-like star in Ophiuchus, 480 parsecs (1,570 light-years) away, whose position in the Gaia satellite's measurements swung every 186 days round a dark companion of 9.6 solar masses: Gaia BH1, the nearest black hole known.[^elbadry2023a] Precise speed measurements, many from the ESPRESSO spectrograph in Chile, have since put it at 9.27.[^nagarajan2024] Gaia BH2 followed in 2023,[^elbadry2023b] and Gaia BH3 in 2024, found while the Gaia team checked data for its next release: 32.7 solar masses, 590 parsecs away in Aquila, the most massive black hole of stellar origin known in the Milky Way.[^panuzzo2024][^esabh3]

A lone black hole can give itself away by bending the light of a star behind it. In 2011 surveys of the crowded fields towards the Galactic Centre saw a star brighten and fade over months, and Kailash Sahu's team used Hubble images from eight visits over six years to measure how far its image was pushed aside. That gave the mass of the dark lens in front: 7.1 solar masses, the first black hole known to be alone.[^sahu2022] Another team found 1.6 to 4.4 from the same event, which would leave room for a neutron star;[^lam2022] with eleven years of Hubble images, Sahu's group now finds 7.15.[^sahu2025]

Black holes can also be heard. At 09:50:45 UTC on 14 September 2015 both LIGO detectors recorded a signal rising in pitch for a fraction of a second: two black holes of 36 and 29 solar masses merging into one of 62 some 1.3 billion years ago, with three solar masses turned into gravitational waves, ripples in space and time.[^gw150914][^ligo2016] Rainer Weiss, Barry Barish and Kip Thorne shared the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics for it,[^nobel2017] and the detectors' fourth run, which ended on 18 November 2025, caught about 250 candidate signals.[^ligoo4]

- **Twenty-four in pairs with a star,** each weighed by its companion's orbit (the twenty-fifth stellar one is OGLE-2011-BLG-0462, alone). A catalogue of them, BlackCAT, lists the black holes found when an outburst of X-rays gave them away;[^blackcat] Skyfold has its confirmed ones with the papers that weighed them. GRS 1915+105, 12 solar masses, shone in X-rays for 26 years from 1992 and has been hidden behind its own gas since 2019;[^reid2014][^motta2021] GRO J0422+32, at about 2.7 solar masses, is one of the lightest black holes known.[^casares2022]
- **Three in other galaxies:** LMC X-1 and LMC X-3 in the Large Magellanic Cloud, 160,000 light-years away, and M33 X-7 in the Triangulum Galaxy, 2.7 million light-years away, a black hole that its giant companion eclipses every 3.45 days.[^orosz2009][^orosz2007]
- **Glowing discs.** Five shine steadily enough to be drawn with a thin disc of hot gas, each a model of a typical state rather than of today: Cygnus X-1, LMC X-1 (at 16% of its Eddington limit), LMC X-3, M33 X-7 and GRS 1915+105 in its bright years.[^gou2009]
- **Fifteen giants at the centres of galaxies,** Sgr A\* and M87\* among them, weighed by the motions of their stars, of gas, or, in NGC 4258, of water masers circling it in a thin disc.[^kormendyho2013][^reid2019] They run from about half a million solar masses in the small galaxy NGC 404 to 21 billion in NGC 4889, in the Coma cluster.[^mcconnell2011] A mass found from motions grows with the distance assumed, so Skyfold scales each to where it places the galaxy.

## What a black hole looks like

Light bends near any mass. At the Sun's edge Einstein predicted a bend of 1.75 arcseconds, twice the Newtonian value, and the eclipse expeditions of May 1919 measured 1.98 in Brazil and 1.61 on Príncipe.[^dyson1920] In 1936 a Czech amateur scientist, Rudi Mandl, pressed Einstein to publish the idea that a star exactly behind another would be seen as a ring; Einstein told the editor of *Science* that the paper was "of little value, but it makes the poor guy happy".[^renn2000] Close to a black hole the bending is extreme. In 1978, at the Paris Observatory in Meudon, Jean-Pierre Luminet computed the first picture of a black hole with a disc of gas, on an IBM 7040, and drew it by hand in a few thousand dots of Indian ink.[^luminet1979][^luminet2019] In 2000 Heino Falcke, Fulvio Melia and Eric Agol predicted that radio telescopes linked across the Earth could see the shadow of Sgr A\*.[^falcke2000]

The numbers below are Schwarzschild's, for Sgr A\* with GRAVITY's 4.297 million solar masses and 8,277 parsecs, as in Skyfold. Without spin, every black hole bends light and slows clocks in the same way at the same number of horizon radii, whatever its mass.

### Clocks that run slow

A clock hovering near a black hole, held in place by a rocket, runs slow compared with one far away:

$$\frac{\Delta\tau}{\Delta t} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{r_s}{r}}$$

In words: the time that passes on the hovering clock, $\Delta\tau$, divided by the time that passes far away, $\Delta t$, is the square root of one minus the horizon radius over your distance from the centre, $r$. ($r$ is defined by the area of the sphere round the hole at that height, $4\pi r^2$.)

Ten horizon radii from Sgr A\*, 0.85 au from its centre, $\sqrt{1 - 0.1} = 0.9487$, so an hour there is 1.054 hours at home. At 1.01 horizon radii, $\sqrt{0.01/1.01} = 0.0995$, so each hour there is ten at home; at the horizon the rate is zero, and an observer who hovers never sees anything cross. Starlight falling in to you is blueshifted by the same factor, and staying put takes a rocket pushing at 3,806 g at ten horizon radii; 1 g is enough 51 au out. [Time dilation is real](#/learn/time-dilation) tells how the same effect was measured on Earth.

### The shadow

Light aimed to pass within $\sqrt{27}\,GM/c^2$ of the centre, about 2.6 horizon radii, is captured; at 1.5 horizon radii, the photon sphere, it can circle the hole, though any nudge sends it in or out. Trace the light reaching your eye backwards, and the directions close to the hole lead to the horizon. A black hole made by a collapsing star sends nothing from there: the star's last light faded long ago. Those directions are the shadow. For someone hovering at $r$ it is a black disc of angular radius $\alpha$, where

$$\sin\alpha = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\frac{r_s}{r}\sqrt{1 - \frac{r_s}{r}}$$

In words: the sine of the shadow's radius is 2.6 times the horizon radius over your distance, times the clock factor above. Close to the horizon, the sky left outside that disc is the slender cone of escaping light that John Synge described in 1966.[^synge1966]

At ten horizon radii, $2.598 \times 0.1 \times 0.9487 = 0.2465$ and $\alpha = 14.27°$, a shadow 28.5° across, where the horizon seen along straight lines would cover 11.5°. At three horizon radii $\sin\alpha = 0.7071$ and the shadow is 90° across; at the photon sphere it is half the sky. Far away the clock factor is 1 and a small angle equals its sine, so the shadow is $\sqrt{27}\,r_s/r$ across, the formula [Our galaxy](#/learn/our-galaxy) uses for the picture from Earth: 53 millionths of an arcsecond for Sgr A\*.

### Rings inside rings

Light from a star exactly behind a black hole comes round every side at once, as a ring. Far from the hole a ray passing at distance $b$ is bent by $2r_s/b$ radians, and to reach you from far behind it must be bent by $b/d$, the angle at which you see it from a distance $d$. Setting the two equal gives the ring's radius:

$$\theta_E = \sqrt{\frac{2r_s}{d}}$$

In words: the Einstein ring's angular radius, in radians, is the square root of twice the horizon radius over your distance from the hole.

From 4,000 au, where the camera stops when you go to Sgr A\*, $2 \times 0.0848/4{,}000 = 4.24 \times 10^{-5}$, whose square root is 0.00651 radians or 0.373° (the exact calculation gives 0.374°): a ring 0.75° across, about one and a half times the width of the full Moon.

Inside the ring there is more. Light passing the far side of the hole is bent the other way, so a second, mirrored copy of the whole sky fills the space between the ring and the shadow. Just outside the shadow's edge lies light that has gone all the way round the hole: another squeezed copy of the sky, with another inside it, each 23 to 25 times thinner than the last. Together they make the photon ring.

### The glowing gas

Sgr A\* is starving. The Event Horizon Telescope's models put the gas it swallows at 5 to 10 billionths of a solar mass a year,[^eht2022v] in a hot, thin flow that glows in radio and infrared light and flickers constantly, with flares on top.[^gravity2020flux] In 2018 GRAVITY saw hot spots in the flares circling the hole at about 30% of the speed of light.[^gravity2018flares]

Skyfold draws the gas as a model: a flow of the kind worked out by Avery Broderick and Abraham Loeb,[^broderick2006] fitted to Sgr A\*'s measured spectrum from radio to infrared and turned the way the flares go round. Its visible light has never been seen, because about 30 magnitudes of dust hide the centre of the Galaxy,[^genzel2010] so it is extrapolated from the infrared and could be three times brighter or fainter; and the model is smooth and steady where the real gas flickers. Sgr A\*'s card can also show the model at 1.3 mm, the Event Horizon Telescope's wavelength, in false colour: a view of the model, not the telescope's picture.

## The two giants

Sgr A\* was weighed by the stars that orbit it, above all S2, which comes within 120 au of it at 7,700 km/s. Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez, who led the two teams that tracked them from the 1990s, shared half the 2020 Nobel Prize; [Our galaxy](#/learn/our-galaxy) tells the story.[^gravity2020][^nobel2020]

::: see-it s2-behind-sgr-a-star
Within a minute S2 swings to its closest to Sgr A\*, 120 au behind it, seen from 300 au out on the far side. Its light comes round both sides of the hole as two images, about 0.8° and 0.7° from the centre, together 5.4 times brighter than S2 alone. Time runs at 30 minutes a second, and S2's own clock loses about a minute a day.
:::

The other giant is 1,500 times heavier and 2,000 times further away, so from Earth it looks a little smaller. On 10 April 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope, radio dishes across the Earth working as one, published the first picture of a black hole's surroundings: a lopsided ring 42 millionths of an arcsecond across round the 6.5-billion-solar-mass black hole in the galaxy M87, 55 million light-years away.[^eso1907][^eht2019vi] Observations in 2018 and 2021 found a ring of the same size within the errors, 43.9 millionths of an arcsecond in all three years, while its brightest part moved round and its polarised light changed pattern.[^eht2024][^eht2025][^mpifr2025] The picture of Sgr A\* followed on 12 May 2022.[^eht2022] [What you would see near the speed of light](#/learn/seeing-near-light-speed) explains why M87\*'s ring is brighter on one side.

:::

Go to M87\* and the camera stops 50 horizon radii out, 6,400 au from it: the same geometry as Sgr A\*'s Einstein ring from 4.24 au, on a scale 1,500 times larger.

## Falling in

What pulls a falling body apart is not gravity itself but the difference between its pull on your head and on your feet, and at the horizon that difference is smaller the bigger the black hole. Across 2 m at the horizon of a black hole of ten solar masses it is about 200 million m/s², 20 million times the Earth's gravity. At Sgr A\*'s horizon it is $1.1 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s², a ten-thousandth of it. Jeremy Schnittman, who made NASA's 2024 films of a fall into a black hole like Sgr A\*, advises: given the choice, fall into a supermassive one.[^nasa2024]

::: see-it hover-at-the-horizon
One per cent above the horizon of Sgr A\*, looking straight up. The black hole fills the whole sky but a disc 14.8° in radius overhead, where everything else is crowded in, ten times bluer. Every hour here is ten at home, and the rocket holding you up pushes at 3.6 million g.
:::

Falling as if dropped from rest far away, you would take 864 s by your own clock to get from ten horizon radii of Sgr A\* to the horizon. Nothing marks the crossing. The dark patch ahead is 84° across, and the rest of the sky is still there round it, pushed forward by your speed. From the horizon to the centre takes another 28 s, and no path can take longer than 66 s. Halfway in, 18 s after the horizon, the dark patch is 107° across; near the end it becomes a floor below you with the whole sky pressed into the half above, which the relativist Andrew Hamilton likens to landing on a flat plane.[^hamilton] Whatever the mass, the tides tear you apart about a tenth of a second before the centre,[^hamilton] where general relativity predicts a singularity and stops working.

::: see-it fall-into-sgr-a-star
A fall into Sgr A\* from ten horizon radii: the first 813 s of your clock play in 20 s, the last 80 s in real time. Drag to look round as you cross. The fall ends where the tides reach 1,000 m/s², 0.03 s before the centre, and resets to where it began. Home's clock is shown on the clocks of free-fallers, a convention: observers hovering outside would say you never cross.
:::

::: myth Fall through the horizon and everything goes dark.
Hamilton calls this a common misconception. The sky shrinks to a small bright circle only for someone hovering; falling freely, you still see all of it round a dark patch ahead as you cross.[^hamilton]
:::

## Hot black holes

In 1971 Hawking proved that the area of a black hole's horizon can never shrink: when two merge, the new horizon is bigger than both old ones together.[^hawking1971] Jacob Bekenstein saw the likeness to entropy, the measure of disorder that never decreases, and argued in 1972 and 1973 that a black hole has an entropy that grows with its horizon's area.[^bekenstein1973] Something with entropy ought to have a temperature, and so to glow, and in 1974 Hawking found that quantum physics makes black holes do just that.[^hawking1974] The temperature is

$$T = \frac{\hbar c^3}{8\pi G M k_B}$$

In words: the temperature is the reduced Planck constant $\hbar$ times the cube of the speed of light, divided by eight times $\pi$ times the gravitational constant, the mass and Boltzmann's constant $k_B$. The heavier the black hole, the colder it is.

For one solar mass, $\hbar c^3 = 2.84 \times 10^{-9}$ in SI units and $8\pi G M k_B = 0.0461$, so $T = 6.2 \times 10^{-8}$ K, 62 billionths of a degree above absolute zero. Sgr A\*, 4.3 million times heavier, is 4.3 million times colder, at $1.4 \times 10^{-14}$ K. The cosmic microwave background, the afterglow of the Big Bang that fills space at 2.7255 K, is 190 trillion times hotter than that, so every known black hole takes in more energy than it gives out. Only a black hole lighter than about 0.6 of the Moon's mass would be hotter than the background and slowly evaporate; one of a solar mass, left alone, would take about $10^{67}$ years. [The edge of reach](#/learn/the-edge-of-reach) looks at that far future. Hawking radiation has never been detected.

Hawking's area rule has now been tested. On 14 January 2025 LIGO caught its clearest merger yet, two black holes of 33.6 and 32.2 solar masses. Their horizons had a total area of about 240,000 km², and the black hole they made had about 400,000 km².[^gw250114][^ligo2025]

## How Skyfold draws a black hole

The geometry is exact. For the black hole whose effect is largest wherever you are, Skyfold works out how far light is bent at each angle from it, in tables of 512 steps rebuilt as you move, and sends every star, the Galaxy's glow, the other galaxies and the microwave background through them, second images and colour shifts included. Clocks, hovering, orbits and falls follow the same geometry. Its pictures match an independent ray tracer on at least 99.99% of the pixels away from an edge.

Some things are models, and the app says so where they show:

- **No spin.** No black hole's spin is measured well enough to draw, so none is drawn. Spin makes a shadow smaller on average by less than about 8%.[^eht2022] Seen as we see Sgr A\*, about 25° from its axis,[^gravity2023] a spin of 0.9 to 0.94 of the maximum would shrink its shadow by about 5 to 7% and shift it by about half a horizon radius; a fast spinner seen edge-on would look up to about 12% narrower in one direction, flattened on one side, though only about 5% smaller on average.[^kerrshadow]
- **The gas and the stars round Sgr A\*.** The flow is a model, and so are the stars within a few parsecs, drawn from published counts, brightnesses and colours; among them only S2, S29, S38 and S55 are real.
- **No dust** is drawn near the black holes, and M87's starlight is a smooth model glow.
- **One lens at a time.** Only the black hole with the largest effect bends light; the others' effects are then far below a pixel.
- **Assumed orientations.** How the orbits of the X-ray binaries are turned on the sky is assumed (all but Cygnus X-1's, taken from its jet), and so is GRS 1915+105's phase; a few masses known only as ranges are drawn at their middles, as their cards say.
- **The discs** of Cygnus X-1 and four other X-ray binaries are models of a typical state, not of today.
- **Contested masses**, as in the table; the Keck group's mass for Sgr A\* is 7.5% below GRAVITY's.[^do2019]

## What comes next

**Films of black holes.** From March 2026 the Event Horizon Telescope spent two months observing M87\*, aiming for its first sequence of images through time and for a measure of its spin; no results have been announced.[^keuper2026] The next-generation EHT plans about ten more dishes for such films, with no construction date.[^ngeht]

**The photon ring.** No telescope has yet resolved the thin ring of light that has gone round a black hole, whose size and shape carry the imprint of its mass and spin.[^johnson2020][^bhex] The Black Hole Explorer (BHEX) would put a radio dish in orbit to extend the EHT far enough to see it. Its team, which tested prototype electronics in December 2025, planned to propose it to NASA's Small Explorer programme, whose 2026 call closed on 9 September, for launch in 2031; no selection has been announced.[^bhex][^haystack2026][^smex2026]

**Sgr A\*'s spin.** The star S301, announced in August 2026, comes within about 12 au of Sgr A\* every 8.7 years, next in 2031. By following it for at least two orbits, with GRAVITY+ and then ESO's Extremely Large Telescope (first test observations due in 2029), the team hopes to measure the black hole's spin within ten years.[^eso2612][^esoelt]

**More sleeping black holes.** Gaia's fourth data release, due on 2 December 2026, includes the individual measurements behind each star, from which El-Badry's team expects dozens more black holes like Gaia BH1.[^esarelease][^elbadry2023a] NASA's Roman Space Telescope, launched on 30 August 2026, will watch the crowded fields towards the Galactic Centre for long lensing events like the one that revealed OGLE-2011-BLG-0462.[^romanblog][^romangbtds]

**More collisions.** The LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA detectors are being upgraded, with a six-month run planned to start in November 2026.[^lvkplan] ESA's LISA, three spacecraft 2.5 million km apart, is planned for launch in 2035 and will hear giant black holes merge.[^lisa]

**Open questions.** How the first black holes grew so fast is not settled: the galaxy GN-z11, seen 435 million years after the Big Bang, already holds one of about 1.6 million solar masses, still growing at about five times the Eddington limit, the rate at which the light of infalling gas starts to hold the rest off.[^maiolino2024] What happens at the singularity, and to what falls in, waits for a theory that joins gravity and quantum physics.
