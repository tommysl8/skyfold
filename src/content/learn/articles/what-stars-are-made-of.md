On 27 October 1859 Gustav Kirchhoff, a physics professor in Heidelberg, sent the Berlin Academy of Sciences a short report about a flame laced with table salt. He had been shining light through it and into a spectroscope, a prism instrument that spreads light into a sharp rainbow, or spectrum. Alone, the salt flame shows two bright yellow lines. Put a powerful lamp behind it and they turn black, exactly where the Sun's spectrum has two dark gaps that astronomers had called D for forty years. Shine sunlight through the flame and the Sun's D lines grow darker and broader.[^kirchhoff1860]

Kirchhoff's conclusion fitted in a sentence. There is sodium in the Sun.[^kirchhoff1860]

Twenty-four years earlier the French philosopher Auguste Comte had picked this question as his example of knowledge out of reach for ever. We might learn the shapes, distances, sizes and motions of the heavenly bodies, he wrote in 1835, "whereas we would never know how to study by any means their chemical composition".[^comte1835] Comte died in September 1857, two years too early to see the flame.[^sepcomte]

## Rainbows with gaps

Early in 1666 Isaac Newton bought a glass prism, darkened his room and let sunlight through a small hole in the shutter, through the prism and onto the far wall. The oblong patch of colours surprised him, and the surprise led him to the idea that white light is a mixture of colours.[^newton1672] He saw no gaps. His opening was a round hole, not a narrow slit, so every colour smeared across its neighbours.[^ames1898]

In 1802 the English scientist William Hyde Wollaston looked at daylight through a narrow crack and a flint-glass prism. The spectrum broke into four colours bordered by dark lines, with two more dark lines inside the green and blue. He took the main ones for the natural boundaries between colours and moved on.[^wollaston1802]

Joseph Fraunhofer had a harder start. Orphaned as a boy, he was apprenticed at twelve to a Munich glassmaker who would not let him read books or go to school on holidays.[^ames1898][^fhg] In 1801 his master's house collapsed on him. He was dug out after many hours, and the rescue brought him to the notice of the Prince-Elector of Bavaria. By the age of 22 he was in charge of the glassworks at Benediktbeuern Abbey.[^fhg]

His problem was practical. To make better telescope lenses he had to know exactly how much each kind of glass bent each colour, and a smear gives you nothing sharp to measure. Around 1814 he let sunlight through a slit in a window shutter onto a prism mounted on a theodolite, a surveyor's angle-measuring telescope, 24 feet away. He saw "an almost countless number of strong and weak vertical lines", some of them nearly black, and counted about 574 between the lines he labelled B and H.[^fraunhofer1817] His letters stuck: Kirchhoff's sodium lines are D because Fraunhofer called them D.

The light of Venus, only reflected sunlight, showed the same lines. Sirius showed three broad bands that looked nothing like the Sun's pattern, the first sign that stars are not all alike. And the bright yellow line in lamplight, he noted, falls exactly where the dark line D is.[^fraunhofer1817] Nobody could explain that coincidence for more than four decades.

Fraunhofer died of tuberculosis in 1826, aged 39.[^fhg] His tomb in Munich says *Approximavit sidera*: he brought the stars closer.[^ames1898] On 18 September 2026, two centuries after his death, his restored glassworks reopened after storm damage.[^fhg2026]

::: myth Fraunhofer discovered the dark lines in sunlight.
Wollaston saw several of them in 1802 and took them for boundaries between colours. Fraunhofer, working independently a dozen years later, was the first to map hundreds of them, show they belong to sunlight itself and use them as measuring marks.[^wollaston1802][^ames1898]
:::

## Fingerprints in a flame

In the 1850s the Heidelberg chemist Robert Bunsen wanted to identify metals by the colours they give a flame, so he designed a burner whose own flame is nearly invisible. Kirchhoff suggested sending the coloured light through a prism, and by 1859 the two had a spectroscope.[^shi] Every metal gave its own set of sharp bright lines, in the same places whether it went in as a chloride, a sulphate or a carbonate.[^kb1860]

The method was absurdly sensitive. They exploded 3 mg of sodium chlorate in the far corner of their 60 m³ laboratory, and the yellow sodium line showed up in a flame at the other end and took ten minutes to fade. The eye, they worked out, could pick up less than a third of a millionth of a milligram of the salt.[^kb1860] In 1860 lines that matched no known element turned up in mineral water from Dürkheim; the new metal became caesium, from the Latin for sky blue. In 1861 a mineral called lepidolite gave them rubidium, deep red.[^rsccs][^rscrb]

Kirchhoff's leap was to connect the bright lines with Fraunhofer's dark ones. A glowing gas gives out light only at its own particular wavelengths. Put the same gas, cooler, in front of a brighter source with a smooth rainbow, and it absorbs exactly those wavelengths, cutting dark lines into the rainbow. The Sun is a very hot body wrapped in a cooler atmosphere, so its dark lines are that atmosphere's list of ingredients, printed in negative.[^kirchhoff1860]

Kirchhoff ruled out Earth's air as the culprit: it holds far too little sodium vapour, and the D lines do not strengthen when the Sun is low and its light crosses more air.[^kirchhoff1860] In the same paper he named the ideal object that absorbs every colour falling on it: a black body.[^kirchhoff1860]

Spectrum analysis, Kirchhoff and Bunsen wrote, "opens to chemical research a hitherto completely closed region extending far beyond the limits of the earth and even of the solar system".[^kb1860]

### Why every element has its own lines

The explanation had to wait for quantum physics. The electrons in an atom cannot have any energy they like; they sit on fixed rungs, like a ladder, and every element has a different ladder. When an electron drops to a lower rung, the atom gives out one particle of light, a photon, carrying exactly the energy difference. To climb, it must swallow a photon with exactly that energy. A photon's energy fixes its wavelength, and so its colour:

$$E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$

In words: a photon's energy equals Planck's constant $h$, a fixed number of nature, times the speed of light $c$, divided by the photon's wavelength $\lambda$ (the Greek letter lambda). Shorter wavelength, more energy.

For atoms the handy unit of energy is the electronvolt (eV), what an electron picks up crossing one volt, and wavelengths come in nanometres (nm), billionths of a metre. In those units $hc$ is about 1,240 eV nm. Sodium's D lines sit at 589 nm, so each D photon carries $1{,}240 \div 589 = 2.1$ eV. A sodium atom in the Sun's atmosphere absorbs photons of 2.1 eV and lets those of 2.0 or 2.2 eV straight through.

Hydrogen, with one electron, has a ladder simple enough to write down, as Niels Bohr showed in 1913:[^bohr1913]

$$E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}$$

In words: the energy of hydrogen's electron on rung number $n$ is minus 13.6 electronvolts divided by $n$ squared. The minus sign means the electron is trapped: you have to add energy to set it free.

Rung 3 sits at $-13.6 \div 9 = -1.51$ eV and rung 2 at $-13.6 \div 4 = -3.40$ eV. A drop from 3 to 2 releases 1.89 eV, and $1{,}240 \div 1.89 = 656$ nm: deep red, the line astronomers call hydrogen-alpha and Fraunhofer called C. A drop from 4 to 2 gives 2.55 eV and 486 nm, Fraunhofer's F.

Every element leaves its own barcode, and about 25,000 absorption lines have now been identified in the Sun's spectrum.[^fhg] Which lines appear tells you which elements are present. How dark they are depends on how much there is, and on temperature, which tripped astronomers up for sixty years.

## An element from the Sun

On 18 August 1868 the Moon covered the Sun over southern India, and Jules Janssen was waiting at Guntoor with his telescopes. Two pink prominences, huge plumes, stood out from the Sun's edge. His spectroscope broke their light into a few bright lines, the mark of a glowing gas, mostly hydrogen. He also realised that because prominences shine in only a handful of colours, a spectroscope tuned to those colours should show them in daylight. The next day it did, and he went on mapping prominences without any eclipse until 4 September.[^cr1868]

His letter left India on 19 September and was read to the Académie des sciences in Paris on 26 October. At the same session the Académie heard from Norman Lockyer in London, who had proposed the same method in 1866 and made it work on 20 October.[^cr1868][^lockyer1868]

Lockyer's prominence showed three bright lines: one matched Fraunhofer's C, one nearly matched F, and one lay close to D but not on it.[^cr1868][^lockyer1868] The first two were hydrogen. The third matched nothing in any laboratory, and Lockyer decided it belonged to an element unknown on Earth. He named it helium, after *helios*, the Greek for Sun.[^rschelium]

It would have been easy to wave the line away as sodium. It lies at 587.5 nm, against 589.0 and 589.6 nm for sodium's pair, so its photons carry 2.110 eV against sodium's 2.105 eV, a quarter of a per cent apart. A good spectroscope separates them cleanly.

The element stayed in the sky for twenty-seven years. In 1895 William Ramsay, busy with argon, read that the American chemist William Hillebrand had boiled a uranium mineral in acid and collected a gas he took to be nitrogen. Ramsay doubted it. He boiled the mineral cleveite in weak sulphuric acid, sealed the gas in a glass tube and made it glow with an electric current, like a neon sign. Beside the lines of argon and hydrogen there was a brilliant yellow line, "nearly but not quite coincident" with sodium's D. William Crookes measured it at 587.49 nm, exactly the solar line.[^ramsay1895] Helium had been found in the Sun before it was found on Earth.

Janssen's report to Paris described the prominence as mostly hydrogen and said nothing about a new element; his triumph was a way to watch prominences without an eclipse. It was Lockyer who insisted the yellow line was not sodium and named the element behind it.[^cr1868][^rschelium]

## Sorting the stars

On 25 September 1890 Edward Pickering, director of Harvard College Observatory, signed the preface to the Draper Catalogue: the spectra of 10,351 stars from 633 photographic plates, taken through a prism so that one plate sometimes caught more than 200 tiny spectra at once. Henry Draper's widow paid for the work in his memory.[^draper1890] Pickering credited Williamina Fleming with measuring and classifying every spectrum. She sorted the stars into classes labelled A to Q, mostly subdivisions of four types proposed earlier by Angelo Secchi: A for spectra showing nothing but hydrogen lines, M for Secchi's banded third type, and so on.[^draper1890]

Harvard paid teams of women, known as computers, to examine a collection that eventually reached about half a million glass plates taken between 1886 and 1989.[^cfa] One of them, Henrietta Leavitt, found the rule for pulsing stars that became a yardstick for the universe ([Island universes](#/learn/island-universes) tells that story).

Antonia Maury took the bright northern stars, photographed in far more detail, and sorted them into 22 classes on her own.[^maury1897] Annie Jump Cannon took the southern stars. In her 1901 catalogue she kept Fleming's letters but dropped most of them and put the rest in a new order, O, B, A, F, G, K, M, with in-between types written as B5A, later shortened to B5.[^cannon1901] Pickering remarked that the names hardly mattered as long as the sorting was accurate.[^cannon1901] The order turned out to matter a great deal.

Cannon went on to classify the stars of the whole sky down to about ninth or tenth magnitude, far too faint for the naked eye: the Henry Draper Catalogue of 1918 to 1924 gives types for 225,300 stars, and its extension, completed by Margaret Walton Mayall in 1949, carries the numbers past 359,000.[^hd]

Meanwhile, distances measured to nearby stars showed how much light each one gives out ([How far are the stars?](#/learn/how-far-are-the-stars) tells how those distances were won). In 1905 the Danish astronomer Ejnar Hertzsprung noticed that stars of the same colour come in two very different brightnesses. In 1914 Henry Norris Russell at Princeton plotted true brightness against spectral class and found the same two groups, which, he wrote, Hertzsprung had given "the excellent names of giant and dwarf stars".[^russell1914] Most stars, the Sun included, lie along one diagonal band on that plot, the main sequence. Nobody yet knew what the letters were measuring.

### Colour is a thermometer

Heat an iron poker and it glows dull red, then orange, then yellow-white; every solid starts to glow red at about the same temperature, as Kirchhoff noted.[^kirchhoff1860] As an object gets hotter, the peak of its glow slides to shorter, bluer wavelengths. For Kirchhoff's ideal black body, which stars resemble closely enough, the rule is:

$$\lambda_{\text{peak}} = \frac{2.898\ \text{mm K}}{T}$$

In words: the wavelength at which a glowing body shines brightest equals 2.898 millimetre-kelvins divided by its temperature in kelvins, that is, in degrees above absolute zero.[^codata]

The Sun's surface is at 5,772 K,[^iau2015] so its peak is at $2.898 \div 5{,}772 = 0.000502$ mm, or 502 nm, in the blue-green. Betelgeuse, at about 3,600 K, peaks at 805 nm, in the infrared; most of its output is light your eyes cannot see.[^joyce2020] Sirius A, at 9,845 K, peaks at 294 nm, in the ultraviolet.[^bond2017]

Cannon's sequence is a temperature scale, hottest to coolest. The lines change along it because atoms behave differently at different temperatures, not because each class is made of different stuff.

### How bright is a star?

Each square metre of a black body gives out power that rises as the fourth power of its temperature, so doubling the temperature makes each square metre sixteen times brighter. Multiply by the surface area, which grows as the radius squared, and you have the star's total output, its luminosity. Compared with the Sun, whose values carry the symbol $\odot$:

$$\frac{L}{L_\odot} = \left(\frac{R}{R_\odot}\right)^2\left(\frac{T}{T_\odot}\right)^4$$

In words: a star's luminosity in Suns equals its radius in Suns, squared, times its temperature divided by the Sun's temperature, raised to the fourth power.

Sirius A has 1.714 times the Sun's radius, so the first factor is 2.94. Its temperature ratio, $9{,}845 \div 5{,}772 = 1.706$, to the fourth power is 8.47. The product is 24.9 Suns; the measured value is 24.7.[^bond2017] Betelgeuse, 764 times wider than the Sun at 3,600 K, comes out near 88,000 Suns, though its radius depends on its disputed distance.[^joyce2020] Proxima Centauri, 0.154 of the Sun's radius at 3,042 K, gives 0.0018 of the Sun's output.[^kervella2017][^segransan2003]

## A million times too much hydrogen

Temperature explained the order of the classes but raised a harder question. A weak line might mean little of that element, or atoms at the wrong temperature to make that line. Too cool and they sit on the bottom rung; too hot and they lose electrons, becoming ions with a different ladder altogether. In 1920 the Indian physicist Meghnad Saha worked out how the fraction of ionised atoms depends on temperature and pressure.[^saha1920]

Cecilia Payne, an Englishwoman who had studied physics at Cambridge, moved to Harvard after meeting its director, Harlow Shapley.[^aps2015] She combined Harvard's plates with Saha's theory to turn line strengths into amounts. Take hydrogen. Its visible lines all start from rung 2, and in the Sun's atmosphere, at about 5,700 K, only one hydrogen atom in roughly 200 million sits there.[^aps2015] Modest-looking hydrogen lines therefore meant a colossal amount of hydrogen.

Her thesis, finished on 1 January 1925, found the heavier elements in stars in much the same proportions as in Earth's crust, and the same from star to star.[^aps2015][^payne1925] Hydrogen and helium broke the pattern: her table put hydrogen about a million times ahead of silicon or iron. Russell, the most influential astronomer in America, told her it could not be right, and she softened the result.[^aps2015] "The enormous abundance derived for these elements in the stellar atmosphere is almost certainly not real," the published thesis says.[^payne1925]

It was real. In 1929 Russell's own analysis of the Sun's atmosphere also found hydrogen overwhelmingly abundant.[^russell1929][^cfa] Payne had earned the first PhD in astronomy from Radcliffe College, a thesis Otto Struve later called the most brilliant ever written in astronomy. In 1956 she became the first woman promoted to full professor in Harvard's Faculty of Arts and Sciences, and then the first woman to chair a Harvard department.[^cfa]

Her numbers were rough, and she said so: the method was good only to about a factor of ten.[^payne1925] Her ratio of hydrogen to iron, over a million to one, overshoots the modern value of about 35,000 to one.[^asplund2021] On the point Russell had made her soften, that stars are mostly hydrogen, she was right.

## A furnace with no fuel

On 24 August 1920 Arthur Eddington, Plumian Professor of Astronomy at Cambridge, opened the physics section of the British Association meeting in Cardiff with an address on what keeps the stars shining.[^eddington1920] He started by dismissing the standard answer, gravity.

Hermann von Helmholtz had shown that a Sun slowly shrinking under its own weight could shine for about 30 million years. Biologists needed far longer, and as Hans Bethe put it in 1967, they were right: radioactive dating of the Earth and of meteorites puts the Sun's age near 5 billion years.[^bethe1967]

Eddington called the contraction theory a corpse that only tradition had kept from being buried. Francis Aston at Cambridge had just shown that a helium atom weighs about 1 part in 120 less than the four hydrogen atoms it could be built from, and mass that disappears must come out as energy. If stars built helium from hydrogen, the energy would easily cover their needs, and Ernest Rutherford was already knocking pieces off nuclei in the Cavendish Laboratory. "What is possible in the Cavendish laboratory may not be too difficult in the sun."[^eddington1920]

::: myth The Sun is a giant ball of fire.
Fire is chemistry: fuel combining with oxygen. If the Sun's whole mass were coal, burning fast enough to match its present output, it would be gone in about 5,000 years. The Sun is about 4.6 billion years old.[^nasasun] Nuclear fusion releases roughly 20 million times more energy per kilogram than burning coal, and it needs no oxygen at all.
:::

### Weighing sunlight

When four hydrogen nuclei end up as one helium nucleus, about 0.7% of their mass is gone, turned into energy:

$$E = mc^2$$

In words: energy equals mass times the speed of light squared. Because $c^2$ is an enormous number, a little mass buys a great deal of energy.

The Sun's output is $3.828 \times 10^{26}$ watts,[^iau2015] that many joules every second. Divide by $c^2 = (2.998 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s})^2 = 8.99 \times 10^{16}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$ and you get $4.26 \times 10^9$ kg: the Sun gets 4.26 million tonnes lighter every second. On the scale where one hydrogen atom weighs about 1 (atomic mass units), four hydrogen atoms weigh 4.0313 and one helium atom 4.0026, a loss of 0.71%, so the Sun must fuse about 600 million tonnes of hydrogen a second.

That sounds ruinous and is not. Over 4.6 billion years at today's rate, the Sun has turned about 100 Earth masses into light, 0.03% of itself. What has changed is the middle, where the hydrogen is down to 35% by mass, against 74% at the surface.[^bs2005]

## Tunnelling, and a conference in Washington

Eddington's idea had a hole in it. Protons repel each other, and by the pre-quantum rules of physics the Sun's centre, at about 15 million degrees, is far too cool for them to get close enough to fuse. Eddington was unrepentant. "We do not argue with the critic who urges that the stars are not hot enough for this process; we tell him to go and find a hotter place," he wrote in 1926.[^eddington1926]

Quantum mechanics rescued him. A particle without the energy to climb a barrier still has a small chance of turning up on the far side, as George Gamow, Ronald Gurney and Edward Condon had shown for radioactive decay. In 1929 Robert Atkinson and Fritz Houtermans applied this tunnelling to stars: only a tiny fraction of nuclei get through, but a star has an enormous number of them.[^bethe1967][^atkinson1929]

The reactions themselves were still missing. In March 1938 Gamow gathered physicists and astrophysicists in Washington, D.C., to hear what the astronomers knew about the insides of stars, and Hans Bethe was in the room.[^bethe1967][^gwu1938] With Charles Critchfield he calculated the slow first step, in which two protons meet and one turns into a neutron. Out come deuterium (heavy hydrogen, a proton and a neutron stuck together), a positron (the electron's positively charged twin) and a neutrino, an almost massless particle.[^bethecritchfield1938] Within months he had the whole picture.[^bethe1939]

There are two routes. In the proton–proton chain, deuterium picks up another proton to make helium-3, and two helium-3 nuclei combine into helium-4, handing back two protons. In the carbon–nitrogen–oxygen cycle, a carbon-12 nucleus swallows four protons one at a time, passing through isotopes of nitrogen and oxygen (versions of those elements with extra or missing neutrons), then spits out a helium-4 nucleus and is carbon-12 again: carbon is only a catalyst. Carl Friedrich von Weizsäcker found the cycle independently but did not work out how fast it runs.[^bethe1967]

Then Bethe took a wrong turn. His numbers made the carbon cycle the Sun's main engine.[^bethe1967] Better reaction rates and a better model of the Sun tipped the balance the other way: the proton–proton chain makes about 99% of the Sun's power and the carbon cycle about 1%, though the carbon cycle dominates in stars more than about 1.3 times the Sun's mass.[^borexino2020] Bethe won the 1967 Nobel Prize in Physics.[^nobel1967]

## Cooking the elements

Bethe's reactions explain the Sun's light, not the oxygen in your lungs. Hydrogen and most helium are older than any star, made in the hot early universe ([The expanding universe](#/learn/the-expanding-universe) tells that story), which produced little else.[^bethe1967] Everything else needed a kitchen.

The obstacle is two missing steps: no stable nucleus has 5 or 8 particles, so helium cannot simply gain one proton or one more helium nucleus.[^fowler1983] Edwin Salpeter pointed out in 1952 that in a hot, dense core two helium nuclei can briefly form beryllium-8, and occasionally a third arrives before it falls apart.[^bethe1967] Fred Hoyle calculated that this would still be far too slow unless the carbon-12 nucleus had an excited state, a rung on its own energy ladder, at just the right height. Early in 1953 he turned up at Caltech's Kellogg Radiation Laboratory and asked whether it existed. Ward Whaling's group looked and found it almost exactly where Hoyle had said, at 7.65 MeV (million electronvolts).[^fowler1983][^dunbar1953]

In 1957 Margaret Burbidge, Geoffrey Burbidge, William Fowler and Hoyle published a 104-page paper, known as B²FH, that laid out the whole run, and Alastair Cameron reached many of the same conclusions independently.[^b2fh][^cameron1957] Stars burn hydrogen to helium, helium to carbon and oxygen, and in heavy stars carbon, neon, oxygen and silicon in turn, up to the elements around iron. Nuclei with about 56 particles are the most tightly bound, so fusing beyond them costs energy instead of releasing it.[^fowler1983] Heavier elements are built by adding neutrons, which feel no electric repulsion: slowly in giant stars, rapidly in violent explosions, all the way to uranium.[^b2fh][^fowler1983] In August 2017 astronomers watched one such explosion, when two neutron stars merged 130 million light-years away and scattered freshly made gold and platinum into space.[^eso1733]

Fowler shared the 1983 Nobel Prize in Physics with Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar; Hoyle was left out.[^nobel1983] In his lecture Fowler reminded the audience that apart from hydrogen they were mostly oxygen and carbon, and so "truly and literally a little bit of stardust".[^fowler1983]

## Ghosts from the core

Every helium nucleus the Sun makes comes with two neutrinos, particles so aloof that almost all of them pass straight through the Sun, the Earth and you. They leave the core in about two seconds, so they report on it almost live.[^davis2002]

On 11 August 1967 Raymond Davis, a chemist at Brookhaven National Laboratory, wrote to Willy Fowler with the first result from a detector 1,478 m underground in the Homestake Gold Mine in Lead, South Dakota. It was a tank of 378,000 litres of perchloroethylene, dry-cleaning fluid, whose chlorine atoms very occasionally swallow a solar neutrino and become radioactive argon-37. John Bahcall's model of the Sun predicted 4 to 11 argon atoms a day in the whole tank, to be flushed out and counted one by one. "We are ready now, turn on the sun," Davis wrote.[^davis2002]

The count came out at about a third of the prediction. Davis titled the 1968 paper "Search for neutrinos from the Sun", which, he later joked, is how you say that nothing was found.[^davis2002][^davis1968] Over some thirty years the tank caught around 2,000 solar neutrinos, and the shortfall never went away.[^nobel2002] Either the Sun's core was not what everyone thought, or neutrinos did something nobody expected.

The next clue came from Japan. At 07:35:35 UT on 23 February 1987 Kamiokande, a water tank watched by light sensors, recorded 11 neutrinos in 13 seconds from a star that had exploded in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy next to ours; a detector in the United States caught 8 more, before anyone had seen the explosion in a telescope.[^hirata1987][^bionta1987] In 1989 Kamiokande confirmed the solar shortfall too, catching about half the predicted number.[^hirata1989]

The answer came from Canada. The Sudbury Neutrino Observatory, 2 km down a nickel mine in Ontario, held 1,000 tonnes of heavy water, water made with deuterium instead of ordinary hydrogen, which let it count electron-type neutrinos, the only kind the Sun makes, and all three types together.[^nobel2015pop] In 2001 and 2002 the verdict was clear: only about a third of the neutrinos arriving from the Sun were still electron-type, but the total matched the solar model.[^sno2002] The Sun was fine. The neutrinos were changing identity on the way, which is possible only if they have mass. Davis and Masatoshi Koshiba of Kamiokande shared half of the 2002 Nobel Prize; Takaaki Kajita and Arthur McDonald shared the 2015 one.[^nobel2002][^nobel2015pop]

The Borexino detector under the Gran Sasso mountains in Italy has since caught neutrinos from the first step of the proton–proton chain, in 2014, and from the carbon cycle, in 2020.[^borexino2014][^borexino2020] The Sun makes about $1.8 \times 10^{38}$ neutrinos a second.[^davis2002] Spread over a sphere the size of Earth's orbit, that is about 65 billion through every square centimetre of you every second,[^nobel2015pop] and your body stops one roughly once in a lifetime.[^davis2002]

## How stars live and die

Mass decides almost everything. A heavier star squeezes its core harder, runs its reactions faster and burns out sooner. For lighter stars the lifetime falls roughly as the third or fourth power of mass: about 10 billion years for the Sun, trillions for a red dwarf a tenth of its mass.[^adams1997] A star ten times the Sun's mass is thousands of times as bright, so it burns each kilogram of fuel hundreds of times faster and lives only tens of millions of years.[^bethe1967]

In about another 5 billion years the Sun will begin to swell into a red giant, and about 8 billion years from now it will have shrunk to a white dwarf, a dense cinder like Sirius B.[^schroder2008] Heavy stars fuse element after element until their cores reach iron, which cannot pay its way. The core then collapses into a neutron star or a black hole, and the rest of the star is blown into space as a supernova.[^montarges2021][^fowler1983]

## Betelgeuse blinks

In October 2019 Betelgeuse, the red supergiant on Orion's shoulder, began to fade. By mid-February 2020 it had lost more than two-thirds of its usual brightness, reaching magnitude 1.614 against a normal range of about 0.1 to 1.0 (the magnitude scale runs backwards: bigger numbers mean fainter).[^nasa2020betel][^montarges2021] Social media decided it was about to explode.

Images from ESO's Very Large Telescope showed the star's southern half ten times darker than usual: a patch of its surface had cooled and a cloud of dust had condensed in front of it.[^montarges2021][^eso2109] Hubble spectra showed where the dust came from. In 2019 Betelgeuse had thrown off part of its visible surface, several times the mass of our Moon, and its regular 400-day pulsation stopped, at least for a while.[^nasa2022betel]

::: myth Betelgeuse's Great Dimming meant it was about to explode.
The dimming was a dust cloud made from material the star threw off. Most models find Betelgeuse still burning helium in its core, and one detailed modelling study put the explosion around 100,000 years away.[^eso2109][^joyce2020][^physorg2020] A 2023 study that read its pulsations differently argued it is much further along, so the date is not settled.[^saio2023]
:::

Betelgeuse is somewhere between about 550 and 720 light-years away; the distance is still disputed.[^joyce2020][^montarges2021] It also has company. In July 2025 a team led by Steve Howell at NASA Ames reported an image of what is probably a faint companion star, taken with the Gemini North telescope, right where more than a century of measurements of Betelgeuse's brightness and motion had predicted. They named it Siwarha, "her bracelet". It swings back to its widest separation from the star in November 2027, the next good chance to confirm and measure it.[^howell2025]

::: see-it fly:betelgeuse
A flight to Betelgeuse holding 1 g, a push equal to Earth's gravity, takes about 12 years on the ship's clock while five centuries or more pass at home. Put in the Sun's place, the star would reach beyond the orbit of Mars.[^joyce2020][^nasa2022betel] It will almost certainly still be there when you arrive.
:::

## Monsters up close

Interferometers, which combine the light of telescopes metres apart, can now see the faces of a few dozen stars. They look nothing like neat balls of light. Where the Sun's surface is a mesh of millions of small convection cells, red supergiants such as Betelgeuse and Antares show a handful of giant ones, each a good fraction of the star across, which change over months.[^chiavassa2010][^ohnaka2017] Their edges are far darker than their centres, because we see into cooler, higher layers there.[^claret2011]

Stars that spin fast are squashed. Altair turns once in under nine hours, at 92% of the speed that would tear it apart. The CHARA array imaged it in 2007: a quarter wider at the equator than pole to pole, its poles 1,600 K hotter than its equator, because gravity is weaker where the spin flings the surface outwards (the effect Hugo von Zeipel predicted in 1924).[^monnier2007] Achernar is flatter still, 35% wider at its equator.[^domiciano2014]

The biggest stars are hard to measure. Stephenson 2-18 is often called the largest known, about 2,150 times the Sun's radius, but that figure rests on a temperature from a model of its dust and on the distance of a star cluster it may not belong to; nearer, it would be smaller.[^fok2012][^humphreys2020] UY Scuti was billed at 1,708 solar radii until Gaia measured its distance; the same angular size then makes it about 1,100.[^arroyo2013]

A tour of the extreme stars, each drawn as it is measured: Betelgeuse's giant cells, Stephenson 2-18 and UY Scuti, Altair and Achernar squashed by their spin, Eta Carinae inside the Homunculus it threw off in the 1840s, and the dust spiral of WR 104 turning once every 241.5 days.[^smith2006][^tuthill2008]
:::

## What comes next

### A ten-year film of the changing sky

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile issued its first scientific alerts on the night of 24 February 2026 and at the end of June 2026 began the Legacy Survey of Space and Time. It will photograph each patch of the southern sky about 800 times over ten years and send out as many as seven million alerts a night about flaring stars, pulsating stars and supernovae.[^noirlab2605][^noirlab2616]

**Status:** running since the end of June 2026 (announced on 30 June); planned to end around 2036.

### Chemistry for 36 million stars

ESA's star-mapping Gaia spacecraft has finished observing, and its fourth data release, built from 66 months of data, will list abundances of chemical elements for 35,825,564 stars, the largest chemical census of the Milky Way yet.[^gaiadr4]

**Status:** scheduled for 2 December 2026; the final release is not expected before the end of 2030.

### Listening to starquakes

Sound waves inside a star make its surface flicker slightly, and the pattern of flickers reveals what the star is like inside. ESA's PLATO mission will watch more than 200,000 stars with 26 cameras, looking for Earth-sized planets and measuring these flickers in their host stars.[^plato] [Other worlds](#/learn/other-worlds) follows the planet hunt.

**Status:** in final testing; launch planned for March 2027 on an Ariane 6.

### The first stars

The very first stars formed from pure hydrogen and helium and should have been huge and short-lived; none has been seen for certain. In 2023 a James Webb Space Telescope team reported helium light with no sign of heavier elements in a clump near the galaxy GN-z11, possibly from such stars, though other explanations remain.[^maiolino2024] On 16 March 2026 astronomers reported PicII-503, a star in the tiny ancient galaxy Pictor II with less than 1/40,000 of the Sun's iron, whose chemistry preserves the leftovers of one of the first stars.[^noirlab2607]

**Status:** candidates, not confirmed detections; Webb and Rubin surveys continue.

::: see-it go:gn-z11
GN-z11 is seen as it was when the universe was about 435 million years old, 3% of its present age. The helium-rich clump lies just beside it, too small and faint to draw here.
:::

### The next supernova in the Milky Way

No supernova has been seen in our galaxy since the 17th century.[^eso2109] One careful combined estimate puts the rate at about 1.6 per century, one every 61 years on average, so the next could come any night, probably hidden behind dust.[^rozwadowska2021] The neutrinos will arrive first, and detectors worldwide have been linked since 1998 in a Supernova Early Warning System, fully automatic since 2005, that raises the alarm when several see a burst at once.[^snews2021]

**Status:** Super-Kamiokande, 50,000 tonnes of water, has run since 1996.[^nobel2015pop] China's JUNO, 20,000 tonnes of liquid scintillator (a liquid that flashes when a particle hits it), began taking data on 26 August 2025 and published its first result on 10 June 2026.[^juno2026] Japan's Hyper-Kamiokande finished digging its main cavern on 31 July 2025; operations were planned for 2027 when it was approved in 2020, but that date is not firm.[^hyperk2020][^hyperk2025] The Deep Underground Neutrino Experiment is being built in the old Homestake mine where Davis ran his tank; detector steel began going underground in May 2026, and the project's milestones give no date yet for the start of physics.[^dune][^surf]

### The Sun's own recipe

Models of the Sun's interior built with the lower amounts of carbon, nitrogen and oxygen measured by Asplund and colleagues do not match helioseismology, the measurements of the Sun's own vibrations, and their 2021 revision leaves this problem "intact".[^asplund2021] A 2022 reanalysis led by Ekaterina Magg found higher amounts that remove the mismatch, so the recipe itself is now in dispute.[^magg2022] Carbon-cycle neutrinos give an independent check, because their rate depends directly on how much carbon, nitrogen and oxygen sit in the core.[^borexino2020]

**Status:** unresolved; better measurements of carbon-cycle neutrinos are the most direct test.
