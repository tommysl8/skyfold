In the most crowded part of the Milky Way, William Herschel once counted 588 stars in a single field of view, a patch of sky half the width of the full Moon. As the sky turned, he reckoned, 116,000 stars passed through it in a quarter of an hour.[^herschel1785] His 20-foot reflector, with a mirror 18.7 inches (47.5 cm) across, was his measuring rod: where he counted more stars, the Milky Way had to reach further into space.

His sister Caroline was his partner in it. On a working night William stood high on the telescope while Caroline sat indoors by a window with the star atlases, a system of strings telling her where he was pointing. She wrote down what he saw and did the calculations that turned his readings into positions.[^lhcaroline] On 3 February 1785 William's paper was read to the Royal Society: a flattened, branching slab of many millions of stars, with a large star near the middle to mark the Sun.[^herschel1785]

It was careful work, and wrong in the way that mattered most. The Sun is nowhere near the middle. Astronomers needed more than 130 years to find that out, and twelve more to see why Herschel could not have known: the Milky Way is full of dust, and in visible light the dust closes off the view a few kiloparsecs away along the disc, whichever way you look.[^okw1958]

A parsec is 3.26 light-years, and a kiloparsec (kpc) is 1,000 of them, about 3,260 light-years. [How far are the stars?](#/learn/how-far-are-the-stars) explains where the unit comes from.
:::

## A river of stars

Aristotle recorded that Anaxagoras and Democritus took the Milky Way to be the light of stars, and argued that it was really a glow in the upper air, like a comet.[^aristotle] Galileo Galilei settled the matter with a telescope that made things look about thirty times nearer. In *Sidereus Nuncius*, printed in Venice in March 1610, he wrote that "the Galaxy is nothing else but a mass of innumerable stars planted together in clusters".[^galileo1610]

Why the stars made a band was another question. Thomas Wright, a self-taught tutor and garden designer near Durham, suggested in 1750 that the stars fill a broad, thin layer with us inside it: look along the layer and stars pile up into a glowing strip, look out of it and you see few.[^lhwright] Immanuel Kant, who knew Wright's book only from a summary in a Hamburg journal, went further in 1755. He argued that the stars are arranged about a common plane, as the planets are, and that the faint oval nebulous stars might be other such systems, so far away that their light is feeble.[^kant1755] The book came out without his name, and its publisher went bankrupt as it appeared, so the book stayed almost unknown for years.[^kant1755] [Island universes](#/learn/island-universes) tells how Kant's guess about the nebulae was finally tested.

Herschel called his counts star gauges, and they rested on two assumptions: that stars are spread fairly evenly, and that his telescope could see to the edge of the system. In his deepest direction he put the edge at 497 times the distance of Sirius, which with Sirius at its modern distance of 2.64 parsecs is about 1,300 parsecs, or 4,300 light-years.[^herschel1785][^hip2007]

Both assumptions were wrong. Stars are not spread evenly, and dust hides everything along the disc beyond a few thousand parsecs, so every direction in the plane looks about as deep as every other. Whoever does the counting finds themselves in the middle.

## The Sun slides off-centre

The same trap caught the Dutch astronomer Jacobus Kapteyn. His 1922 model, built on photographic plates, was a flattened lens of about 47 billion stars, 17 kpc (55,000 light-years) across and five times wider than thick, with the Sun some 650 parsecs from the middle.[^kapteyn1922] Almost in passing he suggested that one day it might be possible to weigh the "dark matter" in the system by its gravity.[^kapteyn1922] He meant dim stars and dark clouds. The phrase would outlive his model by a century.

Harlow Shapley, at Mount Wilson in California, had already reached a very different answer by looking at globular clusters: tight balls of up to millions of stars that mostly lie well away from the Milky Way's band. Using several yardsticks, starting with the pulsating stars inside them, he found distances to 69 clusters, from 6,500 to 67,000 parsecs.[^shapley1918] In three dimensions they sat evenly above and below the plane of the Milky Way, but not around the Sun: they crowded towards Sagittarius. A system that big, Shapley reasoned, must share the centre of the Galaxy, so he put the centre there, provisionally 13,000 parsecs (42,000 light-years) away and probably further.[^shapley1918]

He also saw that no globular cluster lay within 1,300 parsecs of the plane: there was a gap right along the band, where stars are thickest. Among the possible causes he listed clouds of light-absorbing matter, then set that one aside and preferred the idea that clusters could not survive in the crowded plane.[^shapley1918] He was looking straight at the dust.

Both men were partly right. Kapteyn's counts were good, but his lens was only the patch of the disc whose light could reach us. Shapley had the right direction and a distance at least 60% too big, partly because dust dimmed his clusters and made them look further away.[^trumpler1930]

## Dust

Robert Trumpler at Lick Observatory settled it in 1930. He found the distances of 100 open clusters, the loose young clusters in the plane, in two ways: from the brightness of their stars, using the rule that light fades with the square of distance, and from their sizes, assuming clusters of the same type are about the same size.[^trumpler1930] The two disagreed, and not at random: the further away the cluster, the more the brightness method overestimated its distance. Unless clusters grew with their distance from the Sun, something between the stars was absorbing light, dimming it by about 0.7 magnitudes (the brightness scale explained below) for every 1,000 parsecs and reddening it, the way haze reddens a setting Sun.[^trumpler1930]

Kapteyn had measured about the right amount of reddening in 1909, Trumpler pointed out, but Shapley's unreddened globular clusters had "discouraged further work on the subject".[^trumpler1930] The catch, obvious in hindsight, is that the clusters Shapley could see at all lie well away from the dusty plane.

### How much light gets through

Astronomers measure brightness in magnitudes, where bigger numbers mean fainter. One rule is enough here: 5 magnitudes is a factor of exactly 100, so dimming by $A$ magnitudes multiplies the light you receive by $10^{-0.4A}$.

Trumpler's 0.7 magnitudes per kiloparsec lets through $10^{-0.28}$, or 52%, of the light for each kiloparsec. The line of sight to the centre of the Galaxy runs through thick clouds and is far worse: about 30 magnitudes in visible light.[^genzel2010] That is six factors of 100, and $100^6 = 10^{12}$. Of every million million photons of yellow light heading our way from a star near the centre, one arrives. The dimming falls steeply with wavelength, though. At 2.2 micrometres, in the near infrared, it is only about 3 magnitudes, a factor of 16.[^genzel2010] That one fact decided how the centre would be explored: with radio dishes and infrared cameras.

## A disc that spins

A flat disc of stars pulled together by its own gravity has to turn, or it would fall in on itself. In the mid-1920s the Swedish astronomer Bertil Lindblad proposed that the Galaxy rotates, with different parts turning at different speeds. That would explain why globular clusters and some fast stars seem to drift past the Sun at 200 to 300 km/s, all in roughly the same direction.[^oort1927]

Jan Oort at Leiden found the proof in a paper dated 14 April 1927.[^oort1927] If the disc turned like a solid wheel, nearby stars would keep their distances and show no pattern. If it turned like the Solar System, with the inner parts going faster, stars on the inside track would pull ahead of the Sun and those outside would fall behind: a pattern in their motions that repeats twice around the sky. Oort found it, and it pointed to a centre in the same direction as Shapley's clusters. Assuming a speed of 272 km/s for the Sun, he put the centre 5,900 parsecs away, cut to 5,100 in a note added at the printers, and needed at least 80 billion solar masses in the middle to hold the stars in orbit. Nobody could see that much, and his explanation was that most of the Galaxy lay hidden behind obscuring "dark matter".[^oort1927] He meant dust.

### Weighing a galaxy with one speed

For something on a circular orbit, gravity supplies exactly the pull needed to keep bending its path. If the mass inside the orbit is spread in a roughly round way, it acts as if it were all at the centre:

$$v^2 = \frac{GM}{r}$$

In words: the square of the orbital speed equals the gravitational constant times the mass inside the orbit, divided by the orbit's radius. Turned round, $M = v^2 r / G$ weighs whatever is inside an orbit from the speed of anything going round it. For a flattened disc this is only an approximation.

The Sun's circular speed, measured from the motions of more than 23,000 red giant stars, is 229 km/s, and its distance from the centre is 8.18 kpc.[^eilers2019][^gravity2019] In SI units, $r = 8{,}180 \times 3.086 \times 10^{16}$ m $= 2.52 \times 10^{20}$ m, so $M = (229{,}000)^2 \times 2.52 \times 10^{20} / (6.674 \times 10^{-11}) = 1.98 \times 10^{41}$ kg, or $1.0 \times 10^{11}$ solar masses: about 100 billion Suns inside the Sun's orbit. Oort, with the wrong speed and the wrong radius, had the right size.

The orbit is $2\pi r = 1.58 \times 10^{21}$ m long, and at 229 km/s the Sun takes $6.9 \times 10^{15}$ seconds, about 220 million years, to go round. It has made about 21 laps since it formed 4.6 billion years ago. When it started the current one, as the Nobel committee's background notes put it, dinosaurs were walking the Earth.[^nobelpop]

## Listening to hydrogen

In 1933 Karl Jansky, a Bell Laboratories engineer chasing static on overseas radio links, reported a faint hiss at 20.5 MHz coming from the direction of Sagittarius.[^jansky1933][^nraojansky] Radio waves pass through interstellar dust without hindrance, but a hiss carries no distance.[^okw1958] What Oort wanted was a spectral line, radiation at one exact frequency whose Doppler shift would give the speed of its source. In early 1944, in the occupied Netherlands, he raised the problem with a young student from Utrecht, Hendrik van de Hulst. As van de Hulst remembered it, Oort told him that radio astronomy "can really become very important if there were at least one line in the radio spectrum".[^vw2006]

Van de Hulst found one in hydrogen. The proton and electron in a hydrogen atom both spin, and the atom has slightly less energy when the spins point opposite ways. Now and then an atom flips to that lower state and emits a photon at 1,420.4 MHz, a wavelength of 21.1 cm. A single atom waits about 11 million years on average, but there is so much hydrogen that the combined glow can be detected.[^vw2006] He presented the idea in Leiden on 15 April 1944; the war held up publication until December 1945.[^vw2006]

The Dutch search went badly. Their dish was a German Würzburg radar, 7.5 metres across, salvaged after the war and set up at Kootwijk, and on 10 March 1950 a fire destroyed the receiver.[^vw2006][^nraokootwijk] At Harvard, the graduate student Harold Ewen and his supervisor Edward Purcell had a $500 grant and a horn antenna fixed to the outside of the fourth floor of the Lyman Laboratory. It funnelled rainwater into the lab and drew snowballs from passing students.[^nraohorn] On 25 March 1951 it caught the line.[^vw2006]

Purcell then did something generous. Van de Hulst and the Australian Frank Kerr happened to be visiting Harvard, and Purcell asked them to have their home institutes check the result. The Dutch found the line on 11 May 1951, and a team in Sydney had a detection after a six-week crash programme.[^vw2006] The Harvard and Dutch papers and a confirming telegram from Australia appeared side by side in *Nature* on 1 September 1951.[^nature1951]

### Turning speed into a map

The Doppler shift gives the speed of hydrogen along the line of sight:

$$f_{\text{seen}} = f_0 \left(1 - \frac{v}{c}\right)$$

In words: the frequency you receive is the frequency the atom sent, $f_0$, reduced by the fraction $v/c$, where $v$ is the speed at which the gas moves away from you. Gas coming towards you has a negative $v$ and a higher frequency.

For a cloud moving away at 100 km/s, $v/c = 100 / 299{,}792 = 0.000334$, so $f_{\text{seen}} = 1{,}420.406 \times (1 - 0.000334) = 1{,}419.932$ MHz. The shift of 0.474 MHz is easy for a radio receiver to measure. A cloud approaching at 50 km/s shows up at 1,420.643 MHz.

Geometry turns speeds into distances. Point a dish along the plane at 30 degrees from the direction of the centre. Where the line of sight just grazes a ring of orbiting gas, the gas there moves straight along the line of sight and shows the biggest shift. There the radius from the centre meets the line of sight at a right angle, so by trigonometry the ring's radius is $R_0 \sin 30° = 0.5\,R_0$, where $R_0$ is the Sun's distance from the centre: about 4.1 kpc. The biggest shift at each angle gives the rotation speed at each radius, and with that every other shift becomes a distance (inside the Sun's orbit each speed matches two places, near and far, which must be settled some other way).

In 1958 Oort, Kerr and Gart Westerhout joined the Dutch and Australian surveys into a hydrogen map of the whole disc.[^okw1958] Two wedges are empty, pointing towards and away from the centre, where the gas moves across the line of sight and gives no shift. The arms that stood out got names that stuck: Perseus, Sagittarius and Orion, near whose inner edge the Sun sits.[^okw1958]

::: see-it go:orion-nebula
The Orion Nebula, 388 parsecs (1,270 light-years) away by the radio parallaxes of its young stars (an earlier radio measurement gave 414), is a nursery of young, hot stars in our own spiral arm.[^kounkel2017][^menten2007] Glowing clouds like this one, strung along the arms, were what first traced spiral structure in our galaxy in 1951, just before the hydrogen maps.[^okw1958]
:::

::: myth The spiral arms are solid bands of stars that turn with the Galaxy, like the spokes of a wheel.
If they were, they would wind themselves up. Stars 4 kpc from the centre go round in about half the Sun's 220 million years, so arms made of the same stars would soon be wrapped into a tight coil. The classic explanation, from C. C. Lin and Frank Shu in 1964, is that arms are waves of extra density that stars and gas pass through, like a traffic jam that stays put while the cars move on. Gas squeezed in an arm forms new stars, and the brightest die before they get far beyond it, so arms are lined with young blue stars and glowing nebulae.[^linshu1964] Simulations suggest that many arms instead wind up and fade while new ones form, so how long arms last is still argued over.[^dobbs2014]
:::

## The far side

Doppler distances rest on a model of the rotation, and beyond the centre the model is least reliable. The cure is parallax, the small yearly shift in a source's position as the Earth goes round the Sun (the method told in [How far are the stars?](#/learn/how-far-are-the-stars)), applied to masers: dense knots in star-forming clouds where water or methanol molecules amplify microwaves into bright, compact beacons. Radio dishes thousands of kilometres apart, working as one instrument, can see the tiny ellipses that parallax makes them trace over a year.

In 2017 Alberto Sanna's team measured a water maser in the Scutum–Centaurus arm on the far side of the disc, at 20.4 kpc or 66,500 light-years.[^sanna2017] Its parallax is 49 millionths of an arcsecond, the angle covered by a coin 2.3 cm across seen from a quarter of the way to the Moon. In 2019 Mark Reid and colleagues combined about 200 maser parallaxes and found a distance to the centre of 8.15 ± 0.15 kpc, a rotation speed at the Sun of 236 ± 7 km/s, and arms that strongly suggest a spiral with four main arms.[^reid2019]

This is a model, not a photograph. No camera has ever left the Sun's neighbourhood; every view of our galaxy from outside is built from measurements like those in this article. Look for the central bar, the arms, and the Sun about halfway out.
:::

::: note A magnetic galaxy
The disc is threaded by a magnetic field of a few millionths of a gauss, about a hundred-thousandth of the strength of Earth's field at the ground. It shows in radio light: electrons spiralling round the field lines shine polarised across them, and the field twists the polarisation of distant radio sources behind it (Faraday rotation), one way where it points towards us and the other way where it points away. In 2024 Michael Unger and Glennys Farrar fitted models of the large-scale field to both. In the disc the field follows the spiral arms at a pitch of about 11°, the arms' own, and reverses between them; above and below the disc a halo field circles the Galaxy one way in the north and the other way in the south; and an X-shaped field rises through the inner disc, as in other spiral galaxies seen edge-on. A tangled field about as strong again is too small-scale to map.[^uf2024]
:::

## Something heavy in Sagittarius

On 13 and 15 February 1974, Bruce Balick and Robert Brown observed the radio source in Sagittarius with the National Radio Astronomy Observatory's interferometer at Green Bank, West Virginia, which linked dishes there with a 45-foot antenna 35 km away. They found a compact source less than an arcsecond (1/3,600 of a degree) across and argued that it marked the centre of the Galaxy.[^goss2003][^balick1974] Theorists had asked for something like it. In 1971 Donald Lynden-Bell and Martin Rees had compared the violent cores of quasars with the centre of our galaxy and listed tests, among them using long-baseline radio interferometry to "determine the size of any central black hole that there may be in our galaxy".[^goss2003] That test would take until 2022.

The source waited eight years for a name. In 1982 Brown, thinking of it as the source that excited the glowing gas around it, borrowed the notation of his PhD in atomic physics, where an excited atom gets an asterisk, as in He\*. So: Sgr A\*.[^goss2003]

Seeing stars around it took the infrared, where 30 magnitudes of dust shrink to about 3, and it took beating the atmosphere's blur.[^genzel2010] From the early 1990s Reinhard Genzel's group at the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics worked first on ESO's New Technology Telescope in Chile and then on the Very Large Telescope, whose mirrors are more than 8 metres across. Andrea Ghez's group at UCLA used the Keck telescopes on Mauna Kea, nearly 10 metres across, from 1995.[^nobelpop][^do2019] First with speckle imaging, which freezes the air's blur in many exposures a tenth of a second long, and later with adaptive optics, a thin, flexible mirror that undoes the turbulence as it happens, they tracked stars within an arcsecond of Sgr A\*.[^genzel2010][^do2019] By 1998 both teams had found that the closer a star is, the faster it moves, up to about 1,400 km/s, a pattern that held in to about 0.01 parsecs and implied 2 to 3 million solar masses at the centre.[^eckart1996][^ghez1998][^genzel2010]

The clinching star is S2 (S0-2 to the American team), on a long, thin ellipse. In 2002 Genzel's group followed it through its closest approach and published an orbit of 15.2 years; the modern value is 16.05, and both teams have now watched it go all the way round.[^schodel2002][^gravity2020]

### Weighing a black hole with Kepler

Kepler's third law, in units that make the Earth's orbit come out right, weighs whatever sits at the focus of an orbit:

$$M = \frac{a^3}{P^2}$$

In words: the mass at the centre, in solar masses, equals the cube of the orbit's semi-major axis (its average distance) in astronomical units, divided by the square of the period in years. For the Earth round the Sun, $1^3 / 1^2 = 1$. The orbiting star's own mass is small enough to ignore.

GRAVITY, which comes up in the next section, measured S2's semi-major axis on the sky as 0.125058 arcseconds and its period as 16.0455 years.[^gravity2020] At 8,178 parsecs one arcsecond spans 8,178 au, which is what a parsec means, so $a = 0.125058 \times 8{,}178 = 1{,}023$ au and $M = 1{,}023^3 / 16.0455^2 = 1.070 \times 10^9 / 257.5 = 4.16$ million solar masses. The orbit's eccentricity $e$, a measure of how stretched it is (0 for a circle, near 1 for a long, thin ellipse), is 0.8846, so S2's closest approach is $a(1 - e) = 118$ au, about four times Neptune's distance from the Sun.

The distance matters. The size of the orbit grows in proportion to it and the mass goes as its cube, so a 1% error in the distance is a 3% error in the mass: put the centre at 8,277 parsecs and the same orbit gives 4.31 million. No swarm of dim or dead stars could hide that much mass inside S2's orbit: any spread-out mass there is less than 3,000 solar masses, under 0.1% of the total.[^gravity2022] A supermassive black hole is the only known explanation.[^nobel2020]

::: myth The Milky Way turns around its central black hole, which holds the Galaxy together.
Sgr A\* has about 4.3 million solar masses; the Galaxy inside the Sun's orbit has about 100 billion.[^gravity2022][^eilers2019] On its own, the black hole would pull the Sun round at 1.5 km/s, not 230. Its gravity dominates only within a few parsecs of the centre,[^bhg2016] and the Sun would orbit almost exactly as it does if the black hole were not there at all.
:::

## Einstein at the closest approach

S2's closest approach in May 2018 was a test booked 16 years ahead. The star then moves at about 7,700 km/s, 2.6% of the speed of light, deep in the black hole's gravity.[^gravity2018][^gravity2020] Genzel's group and its partners built GRAVITY to catch it. It combines light from the VLT's four big telescopes so that, for measuring positions, they act like one telescope 130 metres across, placing a star relative to Sgr A\* to a few tens of microarcseconds.[^esogravityplus][^esogravity]

Two effects should stretch S2's light at that moment, on top of the ordinary Doppler shift. The star's fast-moving clock runs slow, as told in [Time dilation is real](#/learn/time-dilation), and light climbing out of a gravitational well loses energy. For speeds well below $c$ the two add:

$$z \approx \frac{GM}{rc^2} + \frac{v^2}{2c^2}$$

In words: the extra fractional stretch in wavelength, $z$, is the depth of the gravitational well at the star's distance $r$, in units of $c^2$, plus half the square of the star's speed as a fraction of $c$. Multiplied by $c$, it is quoted as an equivalent speed.

The length $GM/c^2$ is half the event horizon's radius for a black hole that does not spin. For 4.3 million solar masses it is 6.35 million km (for the Sun it would be 1.477 km). At closest approach, about 120 au $= 1.80 \times 10^{10}$ km from the black hole, $GM/(rc^2) = 3.54 \times 10^{-4}$, or 106 km/s.[^gravity2018] With $v = 7{,}700$ km/s, $v/c = 0.0257$ and $v^2/(2c^2) = 3.30 \times 10^{-4}$, or 99 km/s. Together they make about 200 km/s. That is small next to the star's speed along our line of sight, almost 3,800 km/s in March 2018, but it is what GRAVITY's spectra were built to see.[^do2019]

They saw it. On a scale where 0 means Newton and 1 means Einstein, GRAVITY reported $0.90 \pm 0.09 \pm 0.15$ in 2018 (the first $\pm$ is random error, the second allows for errors that would push every measurement the same way) and $1.04 \pm 0.05$ in 2019, and Ghez's team at Keck found $0.88 \pm 0.17$.[^gravity2018][^gravity2019][^do2019] In 2020 GRAVITY reported that S2's orbit does not close: each lap, the ellipse swings forward by 12 arcminutes, the same kind of creep that shifts Mercury's orbit.[^gravity2020] That year Genzel and Ghez shared half the Nobel Prize in Physics "for the discovery of a supermassive compact object at the centre of our galaxy". The other half went to Roger Penrose, for showing that black holes are a robust prediction of general relativity.[^nobel2020]

### The distance to the centre

S2 also measures the distance to the centre by pure geometry: the orbit's size on the sky in arcseconds, and its size in kilometres from the speeds along our line of sight, must describe the same ellipse. In 2019 GRAVITY got $8{,}178 \pm 13 \pm 22$ parsecs, or 26,670 light-years.[^gravity2019] Keck's value that year was $7{,}946 \pm 50 \pm 32$ parsecs, and the gap between the two is about three and a half times their combined quoted uncertainty.[^do2019] Then GRAVITY's own number moved: with a refined analysis and more stars it was $8{,}277 \pm 9$ parsecs in 2022, with about 30 more of systematic uncertainty.[^gravity2022] Each team quotes better than 1%, and the answers differ by 4%.

## A shadow the size of a doughnut on the Moon

Lynden-Bell and Rees's test needed a radio telescope as big as the Earth. The Event Horizon Telescope links observatories including ALMA in Chile, the IRAM 30-metre telescope in Spain and the South Pole Telescope, which record radio waves 1.3 mm long at the same moments so that the recordings can be combined as if they came from one dish. Eight observatories watched Sgr A\* on five nights between 5 and 11 April 2017.[^eht2022]

More than 300 researchers from 80 institutes spent five years turning the data into a picture, shown at simultaneous press conferences on 12 May 2022.[^eso2208] Gas near M87\*, the giant black hole the EHT imaged first, takes days to weeks to go round; around the far smaller Sgr A\* it takes minutes, so the target kept changing while it was being photographed.[^eso2208] The image is an average: a glowing ring $51.8 \pm 2.3$ microarcseconds across round a dark centre, about the size, ESO said, of a doughnut on the Moon.[^eht2022][^eso2208]

### Predicting the size of the ring

Light passing close to a black hole bends so much that some of it circles the hole before escaping. For a black hole that does not spin, the dark shadow in the middle is $2\sqrt{27}$ times $GM/c^2$ across, about 10.4 times. From a distance $D$ it covers an angle

$$\theta \approx \frac{2\sqrt{27}\,GM}{c^2 D}$$

In words: the shadow's angular size, in radians, is about ten times $GM/c^2$ divided by the black hole's distance from us. Spin makes the shadow smaller on average, but by less than about 8%.[^eht2022] Seen about 25° from its axis, as the flares round Sgr A\* suggest we see it, a spin of 0.9 to 0.94 of the maximum would make the shadow about 5 to 7% smaller and shift it sideways by about $GM/c^2$; a fast-spinning black hole seen edge-on looks up to about 12% narrower in one direction, though only about 5% smaller on average.[^gravity2023][^kerrshadow]

For Sgr A\*, $GM/c^2 = 6.35$ million km, so the shadow is $10.39 \times 6.35 \times 10^6 = 6.6 \times 10^7$ km across, about 0.44 au. Dividing by $D = 8{,}178$ parsecs $= 2.52 \times 10^{17}$ km gives $2.61 \times 10^{-10}$ radians, and at 206,265 arcseconds to the radian that is 54 microarcseconds. The EHT measured $48.7 \pm 7.0$.[^eht2022] Stars orbiting 1,000 au out predicted the size of something less than half an astronomical unit across, and the radio picture agreed. In 2024 the collaboration added the view in polarised light, which shows ordered magnetic fields spiralling near the edge, much like those round M87\*.[^eht2024][^eso2406]

Close in, that far-away formula gives way to an exact one, and the whole sky bends into rings round the shadow. [Black holes](#/learn/black-holes) works out what you would see there, and how the other black holes were found.

Ten horizon radii from Sgr A\*, 0.85 au from its centre, the shadow is no longer a speck but 28.5° across, and the stars of the central cluster crowd round its edge, where the bent sky is squeezed.

## A galaxy built from wreckage

Gaia, the European Space Agency's star-mapping satellite, measured the positions of nearly two billion stars, and the motions of most of them, between July 2014 and January 2025.[^esagaia][^esadr3] With so many motions, astronomers could run orbits backwards and sort stars into families.

The halo is the sparse, roughly round swarm of old stars around the disc, and in 2018 two teams found the same family among the halo stars near the Sun. Vasily Belokurov and colleagues, using Gaia's first release and the Sloan Digital Sky Survey, found many of them on long, thin orbits that plunge in and out of the Galaxy. They had been left, the team argued, by a single galaxy of more than ten billion solar masses, dark matter included, that fell in between 8 and 11 billion years ago.[^belokurov2018] Amina Helmi's team, with Gaia's second release, showed that these stars move slightly backwards compared with the disc and have a chemistry unlike that of stars born in the Milky Way. Their parent galaxy, when it fell in about 10 billion years ago, was slightly more massive than the Small Magellanic Cloud, one of our satellite galaxies today, with the young Milky Way about four times heavier, and thirteen of our globular clusters came in with it.[^helmi2018]

Helmi's team named it Gaia-Enceladus, after the Giant of Greek myth, a child of Gaia (the Earth) and Uranus (the sky), who lay buried under Mount Etna causing earthquakes. The parallels were deliberate: a giant among the Milky Way's satellites, buried in the Galaxy and in the Gaia data, and responsible for shaking things up.[^helmi2018] Astronomers now often join the two teams' names as Gaia-Enceladus/Sausage.[^haberle2024] The shaking was real. The merger stirred up the young disc and helped puff part of it into the thick disc of old stars that surrounds the thin disc today.[^helmi2018]

::: see-it go:omega-centauri
Omega Centauri, 5.43 kpc away with about 10 million stars, looks like the Galaxy's biggest globular cluster, but most astronomers think it is the stripped core of a swallowed galaxy, probably Gaia-Enceladus. In 2024, 20 years of Hubble images showed seven stars near its centre moving faster than its escape speed, held by something unseen of at least 8,200 solar masses: most likely a black hole, and if so the nearest massive one known.[^haberle2024]
:::

## The weight of what we cannot see

If most of the Galaxy's mass were in its stars, which crowd towards the middle, orbital speeds far out should fall off, as the planets' speeds do with distance from the Sun. Stars and cold gas together come to about 63 billion solar masses.[^bhg2016] Put all of that at the centre and $v^2 = GM/r$ gives about 100 km/s at 25 kpc.

The real speed is about twice that. Anna-Christina Eilers and colleagues found the rotation speed falling only gently, by about 1.7 km/s per kiloparsec, from 229 km/s at the Sun to about 200 km/s at 25 kpc.[^eilers2019] That speed at that radius needs $2.3 \times 10^{11}$ solar masses inside, nearly four times the mass of the stars and gas. The rest is dark matter, which pulls but neither shines nor blocks light. Kapteyn and Oort used the same name for dim stars and dust; the modern dark matter is spread through a roughly round halo reaching far beyond the disc, and nobody knows what it is. Near the Sun it is thin, about 0.3 GeV per cubic centimetre: a third of a proton's mass in a sugar-cube-sized volume.[^eilers2019]

The total is honestly uncertain. From the motions of distant globular clusters, Laura Watkins and colleagues found about 1.5 trillion solar masses, with a likely range of about 1.1 to 2.3 trillion.[^watkins2019] In 2023 a team using Gaia's third release found the rotation speed dropping by about 30 km/s between 19.5 and 26.5 kpc, and a total of only about 200 billion.[^jiao2023] The two estimates differ by a factor of more than seven. One problem is that $v^2 = GM/r$ assumes steady orbits, and the outer Galaxy is not settled: the Large Magellanic Cloud may weigh 140 billion solar masses by itself, enough to set the outer halo moving.[^erkal2019]

## What comes next

### Gaia Data Release 4

**Status:** scheduled for 2 December 2026, as of September 2026.[^esarelease] Built from 66 months of observations, it covers about 2.8 billion sources and includes the individual measurements behind each star, some 400 terabytes, forty times the size of the last release.[^esadr4] Longer time coverage means better motions for faint and distant stars, which is what tracing old mergers and weighing the halo need. DR5, from all the mission's data, is due not before the end of 2030.[^esarelease]

### The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope

**Status:** launched on 30 August 2026 on a Falcon Heavy and being commissioned. On 15 September its 300-megapixel infrared camera was switched on, and science should begin in early 2027.[^romanblog] Its Galactic Bulge Time Domain Survey will image six fields near the centre of the Galaxy every 12 minutes during six observing seasons, watching hundreds of millions of stars in infrared light that gets through much of the dust. NASA expects hundreds of stellar-mass black holes and more than a thousand planets from it.[^romangbtds] [Other worlds](#/learn/other-worlds) explains how microlensing finds planets.

### Closer to Sgr A\*

**Status:** in progress; completion date uncertain. ESO is upgrading GRAVITY to GRAVITY+ in stages, with better adaptive optics and a laser guide star (an artificial star made by a laser, for the adaptive optics to lock on to) for each of the four big telescopes. All four lasers shone together in a trial run in November 2025, and ESO still gives the upgrade's first-light date only as "TBA".[^esogravityplus][^eso2519] The GRAVITY team expects it to reach beyond magnitude 20 in the infrared, more than 200 times fainter than S2, which may reveal stars on even smaller orbits.[^gravity2022]

Some of that has already arrived. In August 2026 the team announced S301, a star on an 8.7-year orbit that comes closer to Sgr A\* than any star seen before, about 12 au, at some 25,000 km/s, over 8% of the speed of light. Its next close pass is due in 2031, and the team hopes that within ten years it will reveal how fast the black hole spins.[^eso2612][^gravityplus2026] ESO's 39-metre Extremely Large Telescope is scheduled to make its first test observations in 2029, with science instruments following in December 2030; both dates are targets.[^esoelt]

### Movies of the black hole

**Status:** observing continues, and a space mission is proposed. Since 2017 the EHT has added telescopes and doubled the data it records, aiming eventually for movies of the gas moving round Sgr A\*.[^eht2022] The Black Hole Explorer (BHEX) would add a radio dish in space to look for the thin ring of light that has circled a black hole; its team plans to propose it to NASA as a Small Explorer in 2026, aiming for launch in 2031 if it is selected.[^bhex]

### The Square Kilometre Array

**Status:** under construction. SKA-Low in Western Australia released its first image on 17 March 2025 with 1,024 of its planned 131,072 antennas; SKA-Mid in South Africa will have 197 dishes, including MeerKAT, spread over 150 km.[^skaolow][^skamid] Science verification is due late in the second stage of assembly; the observatory gives stages rather than dates.[^skatimeline] Our own galaxy and cosmic magnetism are on its list of science goals, and SKA-Mid is designed to time pulsars, spinning dead stars whose radio pulses tick like clocks.[^skagoals][^skamid]

### Open questions

The masers suggest four main arms, but the far side is still thinly mapped.[^reid2019] GRAVITY's and Keck's distances to the centre disagree by more than their error bars allow.[^gravity2019][^do2019] The total mass is uncertain by a factor of several,[^watkins2019][^jiao2023] nobody knows what the dark matter is, and the Galaxy's long-term future is uncertain too.

::: myth The Milky Way is certain to collide with the Andromeda Galaxy in a few billion years.
With current measurements it is close to a coin toss. In 2025 Till Sawala and colleagues followed the uncertainties in the measurements through to the future and found about a 50% chance that the two galaxies do not merge in the next 10 billion years. The pull of the Large Magellanic Cloud makes a merger less likely; that of the Triangulum Galaxy makes it more likely.[^sawala2025]
:::
